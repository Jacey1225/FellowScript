# Counter-Evidence Plan: FellowScript Marketing Methods

## Purpose

This plan targets the claims in `brief.md` that are most load-bearing for the brief's overall recommendations, and that currently rest on either (a) sourcing thinner than their confident phrasing implies, (b) a single underlying data event reported by multiple secondary write-ups (double-counted as "corroboration"), or (c) an unexamined generalization the brief itself already flags as extrapolation. The goal is not to find "anti-Bible-app" content — it is to genuinely stress-test whether the sourced tactics would actually work for a small, group/study-centric, non-Catholic, non-meditation product like FellowScript.

## Target Claims

### 1. Church/parish/diocese partnerships as a proven distribution channel, including the causal-sounding "30% user increase"

Brief text (Summary §5, first bullet): *"Church/parish/diocese partnerships as a distribution channel. Hallow partnered with 2,500+ parishes; QR codes on parish materials contributed ~9% of overall growth, and 2024 Catholic-organization collaborations correlated with a 30% user increase (Branch.io case study; Contrary Research)."*

**Why targeted:** The "30% user increase" figure comes from exactly one source (Contrary Research, corroboration_count: 1, itself only "found via search summary," not fetched directly). It is stated as a correlation but functions in the brief as if it demonstrates the partnership channel's effectiveness. It is also the single largest piece of evidence recommending FellowScript pursue institutional church partnerships — a channel that requires outreach infrastructure a single-developer team may not have.

**Search angle:** Look for (a) independent critiques or post-mortems of church/parish-partnership growth channels for apps smaller or differently-shaped than Hallow (denominationally broad or non-Catholic apps, since Hallow's parish network is specifically Catholic-diocese infrastructure that may not exist in comparable density for non-Catholic groups); (b) any source noting that Hallow's 2024 growth was concurrent with Lent timing and celebrity ad spend, making the parish-partnership effect hard to isolate from those confounds; (c) accounts from smaller/indie faith-app builders describing parish/church outreach as low-yield or resource-intensive relative to payoff. Where to look: startup/indie-hacker forums (IndieHackers, r/SaaS, r/AppBusiness threads on faith-app or niche-community app growth), church-tech trade press skepticism pieces, any Hallow competitor or critic commentary.

### 2. Church-calendar-timed campaigns (Lent/Easter) "dramatically outperform generic timing"

Brief text (Summary §5, second bullet): *"Church-calendar-timed campaigns dramatically outperform generic timing. Hallow saw 412% install/purchase growth during Lent and 230% during Easter versus 300% from general testing-program gains... February 2024 (Lent) alone drove 2M installs and a brief #1 App Store ranking (Phiture case study; Branch.io case study; Consumer Startups)."*

**Why targeted:** All three "corroborating" sources (Phiture, Branch.io, Consumer Startups) describe the *same underlying Hallow February 2024 campaign* — this is one event reported three times, not three independent replications of the pattern across different apps or years. The brief's own corroboration-count methodology may be overstating independence here. This claim is load-bearing for recommending FellowScript build its marketing calendar around liturgical seasons.

**Search angle:** Look for (a) a second, independent app/case (not Hallow, not the same 2024 event) showing church-calendar-timed campaigns outperforming baseline — ideally a smaller or non-Catholic app, or a different year of Hallow's own data, to test whether the effect replicates or was a one-time confluence of Lent + a Super Bowl ad + a press cycle; (b) commentary on whether liturgical-season timing effects are Catholic-specific (Lent/Ash Wednesday observance is far more universally practiced among Catholics than across the broader Protestant/evangelical/non-denominational audience FellowScript's "community-driven" framing may skew toward); (c) general marketing-seasonality skepticism (regression-to-mean effects in campaign case studies, survivorship bias in publicized growth spikes).

### 3. "Faith audiences respond better to authentic storytelling than slick production" as a governing tone principle

Brief text (Summary §3): *"...independently matches external guidance that faith audiences respond better to authentic storytelling than slick production (Christian Post Advertising; FrontGate Media, creator-partnership piece)."*

**Why targeted:** This claim is used to argue FellowScript's marketing creative should favor reverent, low-production, testimonial content. But the brief's own strongest growth case study — Hallow's Mark Wahlberg Super Bowl spot (Summary §8, sourced from the same Consumer Startups/Branch.io material) — is about as polished and expensive as faith-app marketing gets, and it's credited with pushing Hallow to #1 on the App Store. The two claims sit in unexamined tension within the same brief, and both "authenticity" sources (Christian Post Advertising, FrontGate Media) are agencies with a direct commercial incentive to sell low-cost, "authentic-feeling" creator content rather than expensive celebrity/produced campaigns.

**Search angle:** Look for (a) direct commentary reconciling or contrasting celebrity/high-production faith advertising (Wahlberg-style) against authentic/testimonial faith advertising — does one outperform the other by audience segment or app category (devotional/meditation vs. community/study), or does the evidence just reflect different companies at different budget scales rather than a real content-style effect; (b) independent (non-agency) research on faith-audience ad response — academic marketing research, Barna Group or similar faith-market research firms, or advertising-industry trade press (Adweek, Marketing Dive) covering religious-audience campaigns; (c) whether "authenticity" claims in this space are a self-serving vendor narrative (agencies who can't afford celebrity budgets have obvious reason to claim authenticity beats production).

### 4. Referral-program stats: "~25% higher LTV and 83% higher trust than non-referred users," double-sided rewards as "the dominant proven pattern"

Brief text (Summary §4 and §9, citing Adapty): *"referred users show ~25% higher LTV and 83% higher trust than non-referred users, and double-sided referral rewards are the dominant proven pattern (Adapty)."*

**Why targeted:** This is a widely-repeated marketing statistic whose ultimate primary source is unclear — Adapty is a subscription-infrastructure vendor with a direct commercial interest in customers building referral programs (a feature Adapty's own product likely supports). The brief uses this stat to justify prioritizing referral/group-invite mechanics over paid acquisition — a fairly consequential resource-allocation recommendation for a single-developer team. No non-vendor, non-faith-specific but disinterested source (e.g., peer-reviewed marketing research, a16z/major VC growth analysis, or a company's own disclosed data) was checked against this figure.

**Search angle:** Look for (a) the original/primary source of the "25% higher LTV / 83% higher trust" statistic — is it Nielsen, a specific case study, or does it trace back to a marketing-blog echo chamber with no verifiable underlying study; (b) independent or skeptical treatments of referral-program ROI — cases where double-sided referral incentives underperformed, cannibalized organic growth, or attracted low-quality/low-retention users; (c) whether these consumer-SaaS referral stats (Dropbox/Uber/Airbnb-era) are stale or context-mismatched for a subscription faith-community app in 2026 rather than a 2010s on-demand/storage product.

### 5. The core extrapolation: Hallow's/YouVersion's scale-dependent tactics "transfer" to a small, group/study-centric product

Brief text (Risks, already self-flagged): *"It is not established that these tactics generalize to a smaller, group/study-centric (rather than meditation/devotional-reading) product like FellowScript — this is an extrapolation the brief and downstream stages should treat as a hypothesis, not a proven pattern for FellowScript's specific niche."* Also underpins Summary §8's closing claim that FellowScript's specific feature bundle is *"the clearest basis for differentiated positioning."*

**Why targeted:** This is the single biggest structural risk in the brief — nearly every tactical recommendation in Summary §5 (parish partnerships, calendar timing, verse-sharing virality) is sourced from two apps (Hallow, YouVersion) that are (a) orders of magnitude larger, (b) VC/celebrity-funded, and (c) devotional/meditation- or scale-first products rather than group-study-first products. The brief bounces this into "Open Questions" rather than resolving it, but a scope gate should confirm counter-evidence specifically probes whether feature-differentiation and small-scale positioning is actually a strength or whether it's evidence FellowScript's bundle is an unproven, harder-to-market combination (unlike the case for a single dominant, well-understood channel).

**Search angle:** Look for (a) case studies or commentary specifically about *smaller* or *feature-bundled/multi-purpose* niche community apps (not necessarily faith-specific) where a broad, differentiated feature set became a marketing liability (harder to explain in one sentence, unclear ASO keyword targeting, diluted positioning) rather than an asset — general startup/product-marketing sources on "jack of all trades" positioning risk; (b) any direct commentary on group-study/small-group-specific Bible apps (not devotional/meditation apps) and what actually drives their growth, to see if it differs meaningfully from Hallow/YouVersion's patterns; (c) whether SmartGroups (the brief's own closest competitor comparable) has any documented growth/marketing story that either supports or undercuts the assumption that Hallow/YouVersion tactics transfer to this niche.

## Constraints

Inherited from `research-plan.md`:
- Time period: prioritize current/recent (last 2-3 years) sources; note if a counter-evidence source is meaningfully dated and flag it rather than silently treating it as current.
- Source types: mix of independent commentary, trade press, and (where available) primary company data is acceptable; no requirement for academic sourcing, but disinterested sources should be preferred over vendor/agency blogs when available for these specific claims, since several of the original claims already lean vendor-heavy.
- Do not open or reference `.env`, `*.p8`, `*.pem`, or other credential/key files (not relevant here since this stage is external-only, but carried over for completeness).
- Do not exceed a moderate depth — this is a scoped counter-evidence pass on 5 target claims, not an exhaustive re-litigation of the whole brief.

Specific to counter-evidence:
- A source that merely restates the same original claim (e.g., another article summarizing the same Branch.io/Hallow case study) does **not** count as counter-evidence, even if it appears independent — check whether it traces back to the same underlying event/data before counting it.
- A source arguing "the opposite tactic works better" without evidence is weak counter-evidence; prioritize sources that show a documented case, a plausible mechanism for why the original claim wouldn't generalize, or a critique of the original claim's methodology/sourcing.
- Vendor/agency sources are acceptable as counter-evidence only if they are not the same vendor category as the sources being challenged (e.g., don't use another referral-tool vendor to counter Adapty's referral stats — that's not independent).
- If no genuine counter-evidence exists for a claim after a good-faith search, that absence should be recorded as a finding in its own right (it strengthens the original claim's standing) rather than forced.

## Success Criteria

A good-faith counter-evidence effort for this topic looks like:
- Each of the 5 target claims has at least one search attempt using the specific angle described above, not a generic "[claim] is wrong" search.
- For claims 1, 2, and 4 (the statistic/causation-heavy claims), the effort should attempt to trace the statistic or event to its primary/underlying source, or explicitly note that it could not be traced (that itself is a useful finding).
- For claim 3 (authenticity vs. production), the effort should surface the internal tension with the brief's own Hallow-celebrity-ad data explicitly, even if no external source directly addresses it — this is a case where the counter-evidence may be "found within the brief's own sourcing" rather than requiring new external search.
- For claim 5 (generalization risk), the effort should not just re-confirm "Hallow is big and FellowScript is small" (already known) but should try to find something concrete about smaller/niche/group-study-specific app growth patterns, even from an adjacent (non-faith) niche-community-app category if faith-specific material doesn't exist.
- Where counter-evidence is found, it should be strong enough to actually change a recommendation's confidence level or scope (e.g., "recommend parish partnerships only if X" rather than "parish partnerships unconditionally recommended") — not just noted and discarded.
- Where no counter-evidence is found despite a genuine search, that gap is reported honestly rather than papered over with a weak or tangential source.
