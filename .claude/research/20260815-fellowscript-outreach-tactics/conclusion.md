# Conclusion: FellowScript Gatekeeper Outreach Tactics

## Bottom line

FellowScript should skip the diocese-style institutional playbook and go leader-first: free-tier or pilot offers to individual small-group leaders and pastors, hands-on first-meeting onboarding, and champion-sourcing through existing ministry networks. This is the only tier of tactics with genuine precedent and zero contested claims. Campus-ministry coalitions should be deferred, not pursued, for now — evidence strengthened this call rather than weakening it. Diocese/parish-office partnerships modeled on Hallow or YouVersion carry a heavier compliance and liability burden than a small team should take on before the leader-first motion is proven. No faith-specific product has documented a leader-first analog to Hallow's institutional model; the case for going leader-first instead rests entirely on borrowed EdTech and community-led-growth patterns, which is a hypothesis to test, not a playbook to trust. One originally load-bearing claim did not survive scrutiny as stated: "relational tactics outperform digital advertising" in campus ministry is an overstatement. No source, on either side, ever measured that comparison. The honest version is softer — relational tactics matter more for building initial trust, and relational and digital are complementary, not competing.

## Per-section findings

**Prior-task background carries forward cleanly.** Pastor/leader outreach and best-of-list placement, campus-ministry coalition precedent, Hallow's directional (not literal) growth numbers, and FellowScript's small-team constraint all stood untouched by this round and needed no re-litigation.

**Direct outreach tactics for pastors and small-group leaders held up completely uncontested.** Free-tier and pilot offers work around slow church decision cycles and mirror FellowScript's closest comparable, SmartGroups, which already runs a free single-group tier and a 90-day pilot. Hands-on onboarding at a group's first meeting, done live rather than pushed digitally, is independently recommended. Champion-sourcing through conference networking and existing ministry communities transfers structurally from EdTech. No counter-evidence touched any of this. The one real gap: nobody supplied a literal cold-outreach script or DM template for pastors or leaders — that remains a content gap to fill operationally, not a research failure.

**Pitch angles by gatekeeper role held up on the narrow claims and stayed thin on coverage.** Small-group-leader and pastor pitch material is real and specific. The diocese/parish-administrator claim that available material skews toward compliance and liability rather than persuasion survives for its two cited UK Anglican/GDPR sources specifically — that's not in question. But the broader claim that diocese-facing material is compliance-only everywhere does not survive: a US vendor (Flocknote) shows persuasion-oriented material exists in that market too, though that counter-evidence itself is weak — a single blocked, snippet-only, self-interested source. Net effect: narrow the claim, don't discard it. Ministry-coordinator pitch angles remain essentially undiscovered by either side.

**The core open question — does a leader-first analog to Hallow's/YouVersion's institutional model exist in the faith-app space — comes back negative, and more firmly than before.** No faith-specific precedent was found in either the original sourcing or the adversarial counter-evidence round. YouVersion's early growth was floated as a possible counterexample but is user-first and gatekeeper-free, not leader-first and gatekeeper-dependent — a different problem shape than FellowScript's group-gated model, and the counter-evidence itself concedes this. The leader-first case rests entirely on adjacent, non-faith analogs (EdTech's teacher-first bottom-up adoption pattern, general community-led-growth practice, Notion's Campus Ambassador Program). Those analogs are real and well-documented in their own domains, but adversarial testing surfaced a genuine complication: EdTech's "pilot trap" and the way FERPA-style compliance triggers institutional gatekeeping mid-adoption land at exactly the seam where a champion-led motion can stall once it hits an institution. This doesn't invalidate the EdTech analog — it sharpens the risk that should attach to it. Any leader-first playbook adapted from EdTech needs an explicit caveat: champion-led traction is not guaranteed to survive contact with institutional gatekeeping (diocese review, doctrinal-safety vetting) once it scales past a single group.

**Structural analogs outside the faith-app space (community-led growth, Notion's Ambassador Program) held up uncontested,** but by extension from the EdTech finding above, the same institutional-friction caution plausibly applies to them too — this wasn't tested directly and should be treated as an open consideration rather than a settled point.

**Campus-ministry tactics beyond "relational beats digital" produced the one claim that did not survive as stated.** The comparative framing — relational tactics "outperform" digital advertising — is not supported by any measurement on either side. The best empirical source available (Barna, Gen-Z-wide, not campus-ministry-specific) actively recommends a complementary framing over a binary one. This should be corrected, not dropped: relational tactics still matter more for initial trust-building in this audience, and the right frame going forward is relational-plus-digital, not relational-versus-digital. Separately, EveryCampus's own materials confirm the relational emphasis but reveal the model runs as a 40+-organization coalition — the deeper "how would a small team actually do this" mechanics remain gated and unanswered.

**The prioritized playbook's headline recommendation — defer campus-ministry coalitions — is the best-supported finding in this research.** Adversarial searching specifically aimed at breaking this recommendation (chapter-level blogs, staffer-adoption evidence, denomination-specific searches) instead surfaced independent corroboration that resourced ministries measurably outperform under-resourced ones, reinforcing rather than undermining the case for deferral. The correct framing, though, is "unproven that small teams succeed," not "proven small teams fail" — there is no documented small-team campus-ministry failure case, just an absence of any small-team success case. That absence should be reported honestly as unresolved, not oversold as a negative result. The other playbook tiers (low-effort leader-direct tactics as top priority, moderate-effort champion-enablement as a validate-before-scaling tier, institution-scale diocese partnerships as the highest-effort, lowest near-term tier given team size) stand as originally reported.

**Retention and relationship-building tactics held up completely uncontested.** Periodic leader check-ins, a designated technology-champion role with help-desk-style support, and converting early champions into peer-referral sources are corroborated across independent faith-specific and general-B2B sources. This is the most solidly supported section of the whole brief.

## Confidence

High confidence in the top-line recommendation: prioritize free-tier/pilot leader-direct outreach now, defer campus-ministry coalitions, treat diocese partnerships as a later-stage bet. These held up against genuine adversarial testing, in two cases with the counter-evidence agent's own findings reinforcing rather than weakening the original position.

Moderate confidence in the leader-first-versus-institution-first framing as a strategy, because it rests on cross-domain analogy rather than faith-specific proof. What would change this: discovery of an actual faith-specific app — even a small, niche devotional or Bible-study product below Hallow/YouVersion's scale — that grew leader-by-leader without an institutional sales motion. That single data point, if found, would upgrade the leader-first strategy from hypothesis to precedent. Conversely, if a documented case surfaced of a champion-led faith-app pilot stalling at institutional review (the EdTech "pilot trap" pattern actually manifesting in a church/parish context), that would argue for weighting the diocese-compliance risk more heavily and slowing the leader-first rollout.

Low confidence, deliberately, on the campus-ministry "outperforms" claim as originally worded — it's corrected here, not merely caveated, because no measurement ever supported the comparative framing. Confidence in the softened replacement claim (relational matters more for trust-building; relational and digital are complementary) is moderate, resting on one Gen-Z-wide Barna data point plus practitioner consensus rather than a campus-ministry-specific study.

## Open questions

Every research step from the plan was addressed; three genuinely could not be resolved and should stay open rather than forced to a verdict:

Whether a true faith-specific leader-first precedent exists anywhere — including in smaller or niche devotional apps this research and the prior round never searched — remains unanswered. This is the single most valuable next search if this question resurfaces.

Whether a small team can run any viable campus-ministry outreach motion below coalition scale is unresolved in both directions: no small-team success case and no small-team failure case exists in what was found. The deeper EveryCampus rollout mechanics that might answer this sit behind a gated community platform this research could not access.

The true shape of a US-context diocese or parish-administrator gatekeeper's persuasion criteria (as opposed to compliance criteria) is unresolved. The only US data point (Flocknote) is vendor-sourced, weakly verified, and uncorroborated — not enough to generalize from.

Two smaller gaps carried forward from the brief also remain unfilled: no literal cold-outreach script or template for pastors or small-group leaders was found by either research round, and ministry-coordinator-specific pitch angles remain essentially undiscovered — that role's absence from the literature may itself be informative (coordinators may implement rather than independently decide), but this wasn't tested directly.
