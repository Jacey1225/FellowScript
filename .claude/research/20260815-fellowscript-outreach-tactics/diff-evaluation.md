This is not just "outweighed," the counter-evidence's own honest framing concedes the gap. Not disproven, not false — genuinely holds up, though the deeper open question (does a faith-specific leader-first precedent exist anywhere, including in smaller/niche devotional apps not yet searched) remains unresolved, as the critique explicitly flags.
- **"EdTech analog is a structural pattern worth adapting, trust/doctrinal-safety named but not tested"** — **Stronger side: counter, on the caveat-strength question specifically** (not on whether the underlying mechanism is a valid analog at all, which is unchanged). This is confirmation of a risk the brief under-specified, not refutation of the tactic. Neither claim is disproven or false; the practical effect is that Section 4's "worth adapting" language should carry a materially stronger caveat about champion-dependent results not transferring cleanly and a compliance/doctrinal-review gate being a documented failure point in the analog domain, not merely a speculative one.

## Overall picture

The brief held up well under adversarial pressure. Of the five claims the counter-evidence round specifically targeted, three (no faith-specific leader-first precedent; defer campus-ministry coalitions; UK-context compliance sources are genuinely UK-context) survived intact — two of them with meaningfully increased confidence, because the counter-evidence agent's genuinely adversarial search efforts landed on evidence that reinforced rather than undermined the original position. One claim (relational tactics "outperform" digital advertising) did not survive as originally stated — no source on either side ever measured comparative outperformance, and the counter-evidence's strongest empirical source (Barna) points toward a complementary rather than competitive framing; this should be carried into the conclusion stage in its softened form ("relational matters more for initial trust-building; digital and relational are complementary"), not as a discredited claim to drop, but as a corrected one. One counter-claim (the EdTech "pilot trap" / FERPA-compliance-trigger finding) held up as legitimate, credible complicating evidence that should sharpen — not replace — the brief's existing Risks framing around institutional/doctrinal-safety friction for champion-led adoption.

No claim in either direction was found to be simply **false** in the sense of being contradicted by strong, reliable, uncontested evidence with nothing credible left standing on the other side — the closest case (the "outperform" framing) is better described as an overstatement corrected by softer, complementary evidence than as a claim flatly disproven. Three matters remain genuinely unresolved and should be carried forward honestly rather than forced to a verdict: (1) whether a true faith-specific *leader-first* (not merely user-first) precedent exists anywhere, including in smaller/niche devotional apps this and the prior round didn't search; (2) whether a small team can run a viable below-coalition-scale campus-ministry outreach motion at all, given the persistent absence of both a documented success and a documented failure case; and (3) the true shape of a US-context diocese/parish-administrator gatekeeper's persuasion (as opposed to compliance) criteria, given the one data point found (Flocknote) is vendor-sourced, weakly verified (blocked fetch, snippet-only), and uncorroborated.

The critique itself is thorough, symmetric, and internally consistent — it engaged both sides fairly, credited the counter-evidence agent's genuine falsification attempts where they landed, and pushed back on the counter-evidence where it was weak (Flocknote, Gitnux) or where the original claim was actually right to hold. No bounce is warranted.
