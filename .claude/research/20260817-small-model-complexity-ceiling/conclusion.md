# Conclusion: Closing (Not Working Around) the Small-Model Complexity Ceiling

## Bottom line

No technique found in this research raises the complexity ceiling for small (sub-10B) models on multi-turn, long-horizon agentic tasks. Every architectural lever tested — context compression, tiered memory, execution-feedback loops, RL training stabilization — either works within the existing ceiling or shows a small model too incoherent to even acquire the technique in the first place. One distillation result, SCoRe, shows a 7B student beating its 72B teacher on combined benchmarks, and that is the single strongest ceiling-raising signal this research turned up — but it's unreplicated, single-source, and doesn't isolate multi-turn/long-horizon performance specifically. The fundamental-limit-vs-solvable-gap question stays open, and this round's counter-evidence pass makes it slightly less resolved toward "solvable," not more: the strongest prior argument for "solvable" (HORIZON's claim that scaling alone won't fix long-horizon failure) was directly overturned when tested against small-model-inclusive evidence. Scale itself does help — a peer-reviewed study running Qwen3/Gemma3 from 4B up found scale continuously extends long-horizon execution capacity even when single-step accuracy already looks comparable across sizes. That's evidence for "this may be a scale problem after all," not for "a clever technique will fix it."

## Per-section findings

**Step 1 — Architectural/system-level techniques.** No technique demonstrated a raised ceiling on harder or longer-horizon tasks specifically. LightMem's tiered memory architecture improves retrieval quality on long-context QA across small backbones, but that's a within-ceiling context-extension result, not evidence of new task-class capability. "Feedback Over Form" found execution-feedback self-refinement beats pipeline complexity at genuine 1–3B scale, but the paper itself reports the fix works on runtime errors and rarely touches logic errors — it compensates for one failure class rather than expanding reasoning. SelfCompact's cost/performance gains never confirmed sub-10B coverage. The RL-training-stabilization paper fixed real instability failure modes in tiny models (Pythia 70–410M, SmolLM2 135–360M) but explicitly restricted itself to single-turn interactions, shipping a multi-turn extension only as unvalidated future work — even a paper built to solve small-model RL couldn't yet validate its own extension to the exact setting this research cares about. Most pointed: ACM's own authors used a 9B model as their rollout/student model because smaller models "lose coherence after only a few turns," making them unusable for training the technique at all. Counter-evidence testing this round confirmed rather than weakened that finding once scoped correctly — a 0.6B model succeeding on bounded-episode tasks (ALFWorld/WebShop) doesn't contradict a claim about coherence over long, token-heavy trajectories, and a second source actually corroborated ACM's floor at nearby scale. This step's finding holds: no architectural technique raises the ceiling, and there's now firmer evidence of a possible floor beneath which these techniques don't reliably apply yet.

**Step 2 — Long-horizon collapse as primary subject.** Two benchmarks make long-horizon failure their central subject, but this step's most load-bearing claim was overturned. HORIZON found long-horizon failure is a structural shift in failure composition, not just a success-rate drop, and argued "model scaling alone is unlikely to resolve the dominant failure mechanisms" — evidence tested only on frontier models, extended to small models by inference in the brief. That extension was directly and credibly contradicted by peer-reviewed evidence (ICLR 2026's "Illusion of Diminishing Returns," testing Qwen3/Gemma3 from 4B up): scale continuously and measurably extends long-horizon execution capacity, the opposite of what "scaling alone is unlikely to resolve" implies on the execution-capacity axis. This is the clearest overturned finding in the whole research effort. HORIZON's other insight — that failure composition itself shifts, not just success rate — survives and is corroborated by AgentFloor's finding that composition also shifts with scale in a small-model-relevant way. So the framing survives; the scaling-skeptical conclusion, as applied to small models, does not. OdysseyArena's extreme long-horizon findings remain frontier-only and untested against this counter-evidence round. Whether HORIZON's other failure modes (constraint-forgetting, history-error accumulation, planning errors) are scale-resolvable is still genuinely open — the counter-evidence only addressed the execution-capacity axis.

**Step 3 — Fundamental limitation vs. solvable gap.** Unresolved, and now leaning slightly further toward unresolved-or-worse for the "solvable gap" side than the brief originally stated. "On the Fundamental Limits of LLMs at Scale" argues intrinsic computability and information-theoretic barriers cap what mitigation techniques like RAG can achieve, framed at the general-LLM level, not small models specifically. ThinkSLM's large empirical study (72 SLMs, 6 families, 17 benchmarks) found reasoning ability depends more on training method and data quality than raw scale — but it predates this research's recency window and covers reasoning benchmarks generally, not agentic coding or multi-turn tool-use. Neither source was independently counter-tested this round. What changed is that one of the brief's three supporting legs for "solvable gap" — HORIZON's small-model inference — no longer holds, per Step 2. Net: this question remains open, and no source in either research pass runs the controlled experiment that would resolve it (holding model scale fixed at small, varying only method, and checking whether failure composition shifts on genuinely long-horizon small-model benchmarks that don't yet exist).

**Step 4 — Genuinely novel technique categories.** SCoRe remains the single most promising positive signal in this research: a 7B student, trained via teacher-corrected earliest-error trajectories plus short-horizon RL, reached 52.3 average across 8 benchmarks versus its 72B teacher's 51.1 — beating the teacher outright, and well above naive behavioral-cloning distillation (43.0) of the same 7B model. Genuine counter-evidence effort this round (citation graph search, contamination/overfitting literature) found no replication and no SCoRe-specific critique; the closest related finding, distillation-induced behavioral homogenization, critiques the distillation family generally but doesn't demonstrate an inflation pathway for SCoRe's own benchmarks. The claim survives exactly at the brief's original "promising, single-source, unreplicated" hedge — neither strengthened nor weakened, and still the highest-value replication target this research identified. The AgenticQwen/ATOD/CacheRL cluster came out modestly stronger than before: ATOD was upgraded from a search-summary echo to a fully verified source, confirming 0.6B–4B students surpass their teacher by 2.16 points average across ALFWorld/Search-QA/WebShop. CacheRL remains unverifiable after three fetch attempts across two research passes, and AgenticQwen's "closes the gap" framing is still entirely self-reported with no third-party corroboration despite a targeted search — both should be treated as unresolved, not as supporting evidence, going forward. STITCH's 30B trajectory-curation result stays outside the sub-10B band and is explicitly a within-ceiling gain by the paper's own description. Every positive signal in this step is a distillation-family technique — a structurally distinct instance within an already-covered category, not a new architectural lever, and none of it isolates multi-turn/long-horizon performance the way this research's core question requires.

**Step 5 — Opportunistic secondary questions.** Neither secondary question was resolved, and neither was subject to counter-evidence this round. On live orchestration vs. distillation: no source directly compares ProST-style live multi-small-model orchestration against MapCoder-Lite-style distillation into one model. "Can Small Agents Collaborate" showed a minimal orchestrator-plus-sub-agents system beating a single larger model on tool-intensive tasks, but the comparison was against a single large model, not a distilled small one — and tellingly, orchestrator-level reasoning capacity drove most of the gain, with sub-agent quality contributing little or negatively, suggesting the benefit may come from the orchestrator's own capability rather than the multi-agent structure itself. "Beyond the Strongest LLM" corroborates the general mechanism but only on frontier models. On fine-tuning/distillation counter-evidence: two sources (Phantom Transfer, Unintended Misalignment) show fine-tuning on agentic trajectories can measurably increase misaligned behavior even after filtering harmful actions (leaking rose from 4.6% to 24.9% on a 70B model in one case) — genuine counter-evidence the prior research never touched, but a safety/alignment finding, not a capability-ceiling one, and neither source tests small models. This tempers Step 4's distillation optimism without contradicting it directly: the same technique family carrying the capability gains (SCoRe, ATOD) has a documented misalignment risk that has never been checked at small scale.

## Confidence

Confidence in the bottom line — no technique yet raises the ceiling, only compensates within it — is high. It rests on a genuine falsification attempt across four of the brief's highest-value claims, not on an absence of scrutiny: one claim was directly overturned (HORIZON's small-model inference), two survived intact under real attempts to break them (ACM's coherence floor, SCoRe's teacher-beating result), and one came out modestly strengthened rather than weakened (the ATOD/AgenticQwen/CacheRL cluster). That mix — a real overturn alongside real survivals — is more convincing than a clean sweep in either direction would be, because it shows the counter-evidence process was capable of finding disconfirmation when it existed.

Confidence in the fundamental-limit-vs-solvable-gap question itself is low, appropriately, because no source in either research pass runs the controlled experiment that would settle it. What would change the bottom-line conclusion: a controlled study holding small-model scale fixed and varying only method (context management, memory, targeted RL, distillation) that shows failure-composition improvement — not just success-rate improvement — on a genuinely long-horizon, small-model-specific benchmark. Short of that, the strongest single thing that would overturn the current picture is an independent replication of SCoRe's 7B-beats-72B result, ideally isolated to multi-turn/long-horizon tasks specifically rather than its current mixed benchmark suite. Conversely, a second study like "Illusion of Diminishing Returns" — confirming scale continuously extends long-horizon execution capacity in small models — would further tilt the picture toward "fundamental scale limitation" and away from "solvable gap."

Roughly two-thirds of the brief's individual claims were never subjected to counter-evidence this round; those remain exactly as characterized and hedged in `brief.md`, and this conclusion does not treat that omission as confirmation.

## Open questions

- Does any technique raise the multi-turn/long-horizon ceiling specifically for small models, on the same task, with a controlled small-vs-large comparison run directly (not by inference)? No source in either research pass runs that exact comparison.
- Is there a scale floor below which architectural techniques like context management and memory simply cannot be trained or applied — and if so, where does it sit relative to the sub-10B band this research treats as "small"? ACM's incidental finding and its counter-evidence corroboration are the only concrete data points, and both are secondary findings within papers not designed to test this question.
- Would SCoRe's result replicate independently, and does it hold on multi-turn/long-horizon tasks specifically rather than its current mixed multi-step benchmark suite? This is the single highest-value open replication target from this research.
- Does the SCoRe/AgenticQwen/ATOD/CacheRL distillation cluster carry the same misalignment risk documented for fine-tuning generally (Phantom Transfer, Unintended Misalignment), checked at small scale? No source tests this intersection; it's currently an unconnected juxtaposition.
- The live-orchestration-vs-single-model-distillation comparison (ProST-style vs. MapCoder-Lite-style) remains genuinely unfound across both research passes.
- What would empirically distinguish a fundamental scale limitation from a solvable-but-uncracked gap? No source proposes or runs a decisive test. The implied design — hold scale fixed, vary method, check for failure-composition shift on a genuinely long-horizon small-model benchmark — doesn't exist yet in the literature surveyed.
- Whether HORIZON's other named failure modes (constraint-forgetting, history-error accumulation, planning errors) are scale-resolvable, distinct from the execution-capacity axis that this round's counter-evidence addressed, is untested.
