# Larger-Scale Reach Channels for FellowScript's Leader and Pastor Audience

Task: `20260911-fellowscript-larger-scale-reach` — final conclusion (step 8). Part 2 of a two-part sequence; builds on `20260911-fellowscript-leader-identify-reach-pitch/conclusion.md` (Part 1).

## Bottom line

Larger channels exist, but only one is open to a one- or two-person team today, and that one is a door, not an audience. Planning Center (the most widely used church-management software in the United States) runs a free, self-serve program for outside developers and lists finished integrations in a public directory. Building a Planning Center integration is the best step FellowScript can take now. It puts the app next to a pool the vendor describes as 78,000+ churches. No source shows that churches actually discover apps through that directory, so its real reach is unproven, and it may turn out no better than the small faith.tools listing from Part 1.

RightNow Media (a video Bible-study library that churches buy by subscription) has the largest audience found that matches the target persona, at roughly 20,000 paying churches rather than the 30,000+ it advertises. It has no public way for an outside app to reach those churches. Getting in requires a direct ask and a relationship FellowScript does not yet have. The paid channels (Lifeway, The Gospel Coalition, Christianity Today) are real, but every headline audience number counts something looser than it sounds, and none publishes a price. Facebook groups and app-store featuring are open to anyone but are small or unreliable.

## Background

FellowScript is a Bible study app for the web, iPhone, and Mac. Its core idea is studying scripture together: shared notes, shared highlights, group messaging, and group devotion plans. Its target buyer is the church small-group leader (a volunteer or staff member who runs a weekly study group of roughly 6 to 15 people; earlier research called these "SGPPs") and the pastor who oversees those groups.

Part 1 of this research found ways to reach those people, but all of them were small. faith.tools is a niche directory of Christian apps ranked around 705,000th among all websites, with subscriber counts in the hundreds. Small Group Network (SGN, a membership organization for small-group leaders) runs two conferences, ALIGN and ACCELERATE, that cost $124 to $209 per person and can only reach as many people as fit in the room. Part 1 flagged RightNow Media's claimed 30,000+ churches as a channel it never fully explored.

This run asked one question. Do channels exist that reach the same persona at meaningfully larger scale, and which of them can a lean team act on now versus which require partnerships, money, or size FellowScript does not have? The plan set eight research steps: RightNow Media, denominational networks, curriculum publishers, church-management-software marketplaces, podcasts and YouTube, Facebook groups, app-store placement, and a final sort into actionable-now versus requires-leverage.

The research ran through a fixed sequence. A sourcing pass built a brief with a named source for every claim. A counter-evidence pass then tried to knock down the three claims most likely to be inflated: RightNow Media's reach, Planning Center's install base, and Lifeway's podcast audience. A critique weighed both sides, and a diff evaluation issued a verdict per claim. One caveat runs through everything below. Only those three claims were attacked. Every other finding stands on its original sourcing and was never independently checked. "Uncontested" means nobody tried to disprove it, not that it was verified.

A recurring theme is the gap between what a vendor's number says and what it counts. "Users" can mean everyone who ever registered. "Churches" can include free and dormant accounts. "Reached monthly" can bundle a website, a newsletter, social media, and a podcast into one figure. Much of the value of this run is in pinning those units down.

## Findings by research step

### Step 1: RightNow Media as a channel

The counter-evidence side won on evidence, and the brief's skeptical framing won on interpretation. RightNow Media's own Global Reach page claims 30,000+ subscribing churches and 4.5M+ users. Its own pricing page says 20,000+. The brief flagged the gap but had no independent evidence. The counter side found the only genuinely independent check in the run: MinistryWatch (a watchdog that reads the public tax filings of Christian nonprofits) reported about $35.9M in program revenue from RightNow's Form 990 (the annual return US nonprofits must file and make public). Every other "corroboration" on either side, including the Wesleyan Church, the Learn of Christ review site, and Zippia (a company-data aggregator that listed 32,000), just repeats RightNow's marketing copy.

The counter side then drew the wrong conclusion from its own evidence. It called $35.9M "roughly consistent with 30,000+" churches, which only works if every dollar came from churches paying the lowest price. The critique redid the arithmetic, allowing for RightNow's corporate product (RightNow @Work), conferences, and pricing that scales with church attendance, and landed at roughly 12,000 to 20,000 paying organizations. That matches the pricing page's 20,000+, not the 30,000+ headline. The working figure is now about 20,000+ paying churches (moderate confidence, an estimate rather than a measured finding), with 30,000+ as a ceiling.

The 4.5M+ users figure is settled as cumulative registrations, not active users, at high confidence. RightNow's iPhone app shows roughly 2.5M lifetime installs; adding Android, web, and TV gets to 4.5M only if the number counts everyone who ever signed up.

What held without challenge: RightNow has no public mechanism for an outside app to buy sponsored placement, join a webinar, or appear in its monthly leader email. A pass across its FAQ, launch resources, and partner pages found only one "partner" path, which is becoming a paying church customer. Similarweb (a web-traffic estimator) puts the public marketing site at roughly 991,000 visits a month, but most real usage happens inside church-branded portals, so the website is not the channel. The classification of "requires leverage" holds, and RightNow remains the single most promising requires-leverage candidate: even at 20,000 paying churches it is the largest on-persona subscribing base found.

Two things stay open. No source says what the 20,000 versus 30,000 gap actually measures; paying versus paying-plus-subsidized is the simplest reading but is not stated anywhere. And the price range the critique used in its arithmetic ($1,200 to $5,000+ per church per year) is not cited in the counter-claims file. The 20,000 estimate survives moderate error in that range, but the range should be pinned before anyone quotes "20,000 paying churches" as a finding.

### Step 2: Denominational and cross-church networks

Nothing here was contested, and the brief claimed very little, so this step's evaluation is thin by design rather than by failure. Disciple Leaders Network (DLN, a cross-church network for Sunday School and small-group leaders with Baptist roots) exists, offers free membership to pastors and volunteer leaders, and has a "Become A Partner" section, but publishes no member or church count. Assemblies of God (a large Pentecostal denomination) runs a discipleship initiative called Disciple Well with no published count and no vendor access path. Discipleship.org (a collaborative of disciple-making networks that hosts a national forum) and 3DM Movement (a discipleship network organized into regional hubs of about 30 churches each) likewise publish no aggregate figures and no vendor mechanism.

All four characterizations stand as stated. DLN's "partially actionable" classification holds: it is the only free, on-persona, cross-church network found. Its actual scale and whether its partner program admits software vendors remain unknown. Nobody on either side touched this question.

### Step 3: Curriculum publishers and ministry media beyond RightNow Media

The Gospel Coalition (TGC, a large Reformed evangelical publishing and conference organization) sells display ads, sponsored content, podcast sponsorships, and event sponsorships through an account representative, with no published pricing. Its raw numbers held: 40M "active users" vendor-claimed, about 6M visits a month per Similarweb. What did not hold was the brief's characterization of that gap as a "6-7x" inflation. Six million visits a month is about 72 million a year, so a 40M figure that counts annual unique visitors is a different unit, not a lie. This is reasoning rather than new evidence, but it is sound, and it matters because both the brief and the critique lean on the "TGC pattern" when discounting other vendors. The defensible version of the pattern is "vendor figures use looser units than they sound like," not "vendors inflate sevenfold."

Lifeway (the publishing arm of the Southern Baptist Convention, the largest Protestant denomination in the US) is where the counter side's null result mattered most. Lifeway's ad-sales page claims "200,000+ Christians, leaders, teams, and decision makers reached monthly" across its leadership podcast network. The brief backed that with roughly 2M cumulative downloads for the show 5 Leadership Questions. The counter side could not find independent audience data for any of the three shows Lifeway currently lists (Unseen Leadership, the Ron Edmondson Leadership Podcast, and The One Thing) and, in looking, established that 5 Leadership Questions no longer appears on the roster. That corroboration is disproven: it is evidence about a show that no longer contributes to the network. The critique also read the "200,000+ reached" wording as plausibly bundling podcast, newsletter, website, and social reach, which the brief's own summary had paraphrased as "200,000+ listeners." The literal vendor claim holds exactly as written. The actual monthly podcast audience is unknown; the critique's estimate of "tens of thousands" is reasoned from the Rainer on Leadership range (10,000 to 50,000) and is an order-of-magnitude guess, not a figure. No price was found.

Christianity Today (a major evangelical magazine) and its SmallGroups.com property were uncontested: 4.5M+ leaders a month, 1.25M unique visitors, and 395,000 email subscribers, all vendor-stated. SmallGroups.com is not broken out separately. The unit caution from TGC probably applies here too but was never tested.

The "requires budget" classification holds for all three. What changed is the expected value of a Lifeway spend, not its category.

### Step 4: Church-management-software marketplaces and integrations

ChMS is the software churches use to track members, giving, and volunteer schedules. This step produced the run's one clearly false claim, one disproven claim, and its strongest actionable recommendation.

The brief said there was "no current first-party figure" for Planning Center's customer base. That is false. The counter side found the same statement on three Planning Center product pages: "Trusted by over 78,000 churches." The sourcing pass had stopped at the homepage, which carries no total, and generalized from there. No credible support for the brief's claim remains.

The brief also framed a contest between 73,000 churches (from Apps Run The World, a software-market research firm) and 100,000+ (from faith.tools). That contest is disproven. The critique's independence check shows 73,000, 78,000, and 80,000 are almost certainly the same vendor figure captured at different dates; Apps Run The World lists both 73,000 and 80,000 on one page. The faith.tools 100,000+ figure has no independent basis and fits the inflation pattern Part 1 already documented for that site. There was one vendor number and one bad secondary source, never two independent measurements.

What 78,000 means is the next question. Roughly 373,000 congregations exist in the US, so 78,000 would be about 21% of them. That is a ceiling on organizations with an account, including free-tier, international, and dormant ones, not a count of engaged paying churches. This is a sanity check by reasoning, carried as a confidence downgrade rather than a disproof.

Planning Center's "best actionable-now" designation rests on three legs. Its developer program is free and self-serve: any developer can register an app using OAuth (the standard way an app connects to another service with the user's permission) against a public API (a documented interface for software to talk to software). That holds on primary evidence. A submission path for finished integrations into a public directory exists; that holds as "the on-ramp exists," from a single primary source. The counter side offered a Vanco (a large church-payments vendor) press release as proof that the path works, but the release does not say Vanco used the self-serve path or what a listing yields, so it moves nothing. And Planning Center is the largest in the ChMS set relative to its peers, which G2 and Capterra (software review sites) review volumes confirm.

What does not hold is the brief's argument that install base is why Planning Center beats faith.tools. The integrations directory is a directory listing, the same channel type as faith.tools, attached to a much larger pool. Whether that pool ever looks at the directory is unknown. Nobody found a single first-hand developer account of a church discovering an app through it. So Planning Center is the best actionable-now on-ramp (free, self-serve, largest pool), and whether it is a larger reach channel than faith.tools in practice is unproven.

One unsourced piece of reasoning is worth carrying forward, clearly labeled as such. The critique suggested that a Planning Center Groups integration (syncing a church's small-group rosters into FellowScript) would be useful to FellowScript's own users whether or not the directory sends anyone. That makes the engineering time defensible even if the distribution value is zero. It appears in no source and changes no reach verdict.

The rest of the ChMS field held without challenge. Pushpay (a church giving and engagement platform) claims 14,000+ customers, offers a self-serve developer API, and has no partner program for consumer apps; its claimed second corroboration turned out to be a directory listing repeating the tagline, so its effective corroboration is one source. Church Community Builder (CCB, now part of Pushpay) has about 10,000 churches combined. ChurchTrac and Breeze (smaller ChMS products) run no marketplace. Pushpay stays partially actionable; CCB, ChurchTrac, and Breeze have no scalable mechanism.

### Step 5: Podcasts and YouTube channels

The counter side's Lifeway roster finding undercut this section's headline show. The brief carried Group Answers Podcast (a Lifeway show for small-group leaders) as the most directly on-persona podcast found, sourced from a live Lifeway page for the show. A live page proves the page exists, not that the show is producing episodes, and Group Answers does not appear on Lifeway's current network roster. No source confirms it is discontinued, so this is unresolved rather than disproven. The consequence is real: the brief's only small-group-specific podcast may not be a current sponsorship opportunity, which would mean no small-group-specific podcast channel exists in this run's findings at all.

Everything else held uncontested. Rainer on Leadership (a church-leadership podcast by Thom Rainer, former head of Lifeway) reaches an estimated 10,000 to 50,000 listeners per Feedspot (a podcast and blog ranking service) and has 487 Apple Podcasts ratings. Church Answers, its associated membership community, has 1,800+ members. Discipleship Leaders Podcast has 6 ratings and is tiny. No YouTube channel serving this persona was found by either side. The "requires budget" classification holds. Lifeway's 200,000+ a month remains the only podcast-related number that reaches the plan's bar, and it is now weaker than the brief presented it.

### Step 6: Facebook groups and online communities

This is the run's strongest material, and Part 1's unresolved blocker is now closed with real numbers. Direct reads of the platform's own displayed counters, the most reliable source type in the run, show Small Group Network's Facebook group at 15,200 members with about 19 posts a month and no new members in the past week; Small Group Ministry Network at 420 members; and Church Communications at 38,400 members. Church Communications has rough outside corroboration (30,000 to 35,000 in other mentions) but serves church communications staff, not small-group leaders. Katie Allred's list (a church-communications consultant's roundup of 14 ministry Facebook groups) confirms SGN's is the only small-group-specific group on it. No relevant subreddit, Discord, or Slack community was found. Nothing here was contested.

The open question is conversion. A 15,200-member group with 19 posts a month may or may not outperform a faith.tools listing with subscribers in the hundreds. Member count and reachable audience are not the same thing, and neither side produced evidence on how many of those members would see or act on a post.

### Step 7: App Store and Play Store faith-category placement

The best-corroborated positive finding in the run, from five independent app-store-optimization industry sources (ASO, the practice of getting an app found in app-store search and rankings), and uncontested. Apple's editorial featuring works by nomination, is gated on app quality, cannot be bought, has no faith-specific track, and is slow. It is actionable now and unreliable: a small team can submit a nomination today, but cannot count on anything coming of it. Google Play's faith-category dynamics and any recurring Apple curated "Bible study" collection were not investigated by either side.

### Step 8: Synthesis, actionable-now versus requires-leverage

Both headline recommendations from the brief survive with corrected numbers.

The single best actionable-now candidate is Planning Center, ranked on cost and access, not on install base. The single most promising requires-leverage candidate is RightNow Media, quoted at 20,000+ paying churches, still closable only by asking directly.

## Confidence

Overall confidence in the bottom line is moderate. The recommendation itself is sturdy because it rests on the cheapest, best-sourced facts in the run: Planning Center's developer program is free and public, and RightNow Media has no public vendor path. Confidence is high on the Facebook member counts, the Apple featuring mechanism, Planning Center's 78,000+ figure as a vendor statement, and the reading of RightNow's 4.5M as cumulative. Confidence is moderate on RightNow's 20,000 paying churches, which is an arithmetic estimate built on an uncited price range. Confidence is low on the thing that matters most for the recommendation: whether the Planning Center directory sends any churches to a consumer-facing app at all.

Specific evidence would overturn specific findings. A first-hand account from a small consumer-app developer of installs driven by the Planning Center directory would turn "unproven on-ramp" into "proven channel"; a rejection of a consumer app from that directory would sink it. RightNow Media answering a direct inquiry with a real sponsorship or partner path would move it from requires-leverage to actionable. A published or quoted price from Lifeway, TGC, or Christianity Today inside a lean team's range would upgrade those from requires-budget to actionable. A source stating that RightNow's 30,000 counts paying churches, or a cited price list showing average revenue per church is well under $1,200, would push the paying-church estimate back up toward 30,000. Finding Group Answers Podcast still publishing would restore the one small-group-specific podcast channel.
