# Conclusion: Identifying, Reaching, and Pitching FellowScript to Small-Group Leaders and Pastors

Task: `20260911-fellowscript-leader-identify-reach-pitch`
Stage: evaluating diffs (step 8, conclusion)
Inputs: `research-plan.md`, `diff-evaluation.md` (with `brief.md` for source names and wording)

## Bottom line

The research found concrete, checkable ways to do all three things the plan asked for, and most of them survived a deliberate attempt to knock them down. To find the people who decide, watch church job boards for "Small Groups Pastor" and its title variants; a church posting that job has already decided small groups deserve a paid owner. To reach them, list FellowScript for free on faith.tools (a church-leader-facing app directory) and go to Small Group Network's ALIGN and ACCELERATE training events, which cost $124 to $209 per person. To pitch, offer a free tier for a single group with no credit card, prepare an answer for "we don't need another app," and time outreach around September and January, when leaders are most focused on group logistics.

Three claims came out weaker than the brief first stated them. The "another app" objection is asserted by software vendors, not measured by anyone. faith.tools is a small directory, so the listing is free hygiene rather than a growth channel. SmartGroups, the closest comparable app, has almost no visible traction, so its free-tier structure is sound reasoning but not a proven model to copy. Nothing in the brief was shown to be false.

The most important unresolved item is not a research gap but a product decision. Job boards find churches large enough to pay someone to run small groups. If FellowScript's wedge is small independent churches, that feed is blind to them. If the wedge is groups wherever they are, the feed points at where most groups actually sit. No one has made that call yet.

## Background

FellowScript is a group Bible-study app. Its pricing works per group: one leader signs up, and up to eight members join under that leader's subscription. That makes the small-group leader, or the pastor who oversees small groups, the person whose yes matters. Two earlier research runs settled the strategy questions: grow by referral rather than paid ads, and go after leaders directly rather than church institutions or dioceses. This run was meant to go one layer deeper and answer the tactical questions those runs left open. Who exactly are these leaders, where can a one- or two-person team find them, and what should the pitch say?

A "small group" here means a church-run gathering of roughly five to fifteen people who meet regularly, often in homes, for Bible study and fellowship. Larger churches employ a staff member to run the whole small-group program. The job goes by many titles: Small Groups Pastor, Groups Director, Connections Pastor, Engagement Pastor, or Pastor of Discipleship. Small Group Network (a cross-church association for people in this role) calls them "Small Group Point People," or SGPPs. Below that person sits a coordinator who organizes and supports, and below the coordinator sit the volunteer lay leaders who actually run each group. The plan asked eight specific questions across identifying, reaching, and pitching these people.

The pipeline ran in three stages. A sourcing stage gathered 26 sources and wrote a brief. A critiquing stage tried to disprove eight of the brief's claims with opposing evidence. An evaluation stage then weighed both sides claim by claim. Claims the critique never targeted are marked "untested" below, which means they stand as sourced but were never pushed against. That is a weaker status than "held."

## Findings by research step

### Step 1: How to identify leaders at scale

The strongest finding held. ChurchStaffing.com (a church-specific job board) keeps a live "Small Group Pastor" category that names churches currently hiring for the role. ZipRecruiter (a general job aggregator) shows the same postings with a $42k to $62k salary range. The evaluation noted ZipRecruiter partly syndicates ChurchStaffing's listings, so it confirms the mechanic exists rather than revealing a second set of churches. The category page works as a feed you can check repeatedly even as individual postings expire.

The counter-evidence tried to argue this is the wrong feed for FellowScript's audience. It cited Faith Communities Today (a long-running national survey of US congregations) showing 70% of US congregations have 100 or fewer attendees and would never post such a job. That figure is real and comes from the most reliable source on either side. But the same survey shows those small churches hold only 14% of worshippers. Counted by groups rather than by churches, the job-board feed samples where most groups are. The counter's conclusion rested on the plan's unverified assumption that small independent churches are the wedge. Both sides agree no one has measured how common a paid small-group role is in churches under about 360 attendees.

The title-variant search set (documented by ChurchLeaders, a ministry trade publication) went untested. Using those variants as LinkedIn and job-board search strings remains the brief's own labeled inference. Per-church member directories and public clergy directories went untested too; the brief already scoped them as non-scalable or aimed at the wrong population.

One claim was moved rather than weakened. The brief filed the customer bases of ChurchTrac (church management software claiming 14,000+ churches) and RightNow Media (a video Bible-study library claiming 30,000+ churches and 4 million users) under identification. Neither publishes a customer list, so those bases identify no specific church. They belong under market-size context instead.

### Step 2: Where leaders self-organize online

This step is unresolved by absence. The brief called it the weakest-sourced step, and the critique did not touch it. Named Facebook groups for church leaders exist and are joinable, per a list kept by Katie Allred (a church communications consultant). One is branded "Small Group Network." Most target adjacent niches like children's, worship, or youth ministry. No stage retrieved member counts, activity levels, or access rules for any of them, because Facebook and Reddit block automated fetching and no browser tooling was available. No dedicated subreddit or Discord server for small-group leaders was found. The entire online-community channel remains unevaluated.

### Step 3: Curriculum publishers and small-group networks

The vendor scale figures held as hedged claims. The counter argued that "14,000+ churches" conflicts with "33,000 to 60,000 church leaders," but that is a units mismatch, since one church has several leaders, and the counter conceded it. The counter also cited disagreement over the total size of the church-software market, which says nothing about one vendor's customer count. The evaluation rated the counter's "discrepancy" claim false as stated. The true accuracy of both figures remains unverified, which is where the brief started.

faith.tools was downgraded. The brief called it "the most directly actionable reach item." Its facts held: it is a church-leader-facing directory of 50+ small-group apps, submission is free and self-serve, and paid sponsorship is available. But two independent signals agree the audience is small. A Similarweb (web-traffic estimator) free-tier reading shows a global rank around 705,000 and short visits, and the site's own newsletter tier reports subscribers in the hundreds. The exact traffic numbers are unreliable at that rank, but the direction is clear. The listing is worth doing because it costs nothing and reaches a high-intent reader. Do not buy the paid sponsorship on current evidence.

Three items went untested. RightNow Media sends leaders a monthly "how to lead an effective small group" email; whether it accepts outside content is still unknown. Small Group Network is a real cross-church body aimed at SGPPs, though the two "corroborating" sources for it are two pages of one website. Mark Howell (a veteran groups pastor with 1,100+ blog posts and a ChurchLeaders byline) has a documented audience but no documented willingness to partner.

The brief's correction stands: "LifeGroups.com" is Life.Church's internal branding for its own congregation, not a cross-church network. Lifeway and The Gospel Coalition, both named in the plan, were never sourced.

### Step 4: Conferences and events

Untested. Small Group Network's ALIGN (one day) and ACCELERATE (two days) events target SGPPs by name. Attendee prices come from the organizer's own page: $124 per course, $209 per person for in-person ACCELERATE, or $49 per month for an all-access subscription with half off in-person events. That converts the earlier run's vague "network at conferences" idea into real numbers a small team can budget. Exhibitor and sponsor pricing was not found by any stage, so whether showing up as a vendor is viable stays open. No other small-group-specific conference surfaced.

### Step 5: Cold-outreach scripts

The absence held across every stage. No faith-specific cold-email, direct-message, or first-contact script aimed at pastors or small-group leaders exists in any source set, matching both prior runs. The closest analog is EdTech (education technology) outreach to school districts, where LeadHaste and three other sales-consulting sources give literal template text: a short personalized subject line, 50 to 125 words framed as a note from a peer, one specific pain point, and outcomes rather than features. All four sources sell sales consulting, and the brief labeled them cross-domain.

The mapping of the EdTech "teacher champion" to the volunteer lay leader is unresolved. The counter cited a BMC Psychology systematic review (a study that pools many prior studies) showing volunteers act on internal motivation. The evaluation found that cuts the other way: a volunteer who genuinely likes a tool is at least as credible an endorser as a salaried teacher. The counter's real contribution is turnover. Small-group and youth ministry are described as the highest-turnover volunteer roles, and US formal volunteering fell from 30% to 23.2% between 2019 and 2021. But the faith-specific turnover source is a church-software vendor blog, and the national figure is a pandemic-year reading with no established trend. Carry the risk forward: a group's adoption may lapse when its leader rotates out. The critique also noted a structural difference neither side sourced. In EdTech the teacher cannot buy, since the district holds the budget. In FellowScript's model the leader's yes unlocks the group directly, which makes the analogy harder on procurement and easier on champion durability than FellowScript's actual case.

### Step 6: Pitch messaging and timing

The "another app" objection was downgraded. The brief called tool-fragmentation fatigue a "documented" objection, citing Subsplash (a church app and giving platform). The counter cited a 2026 Barna survey commissioned by Pushpay (Barna is a faith-focused research firm; Pushpay sells church software) showing 95% of church leaders say technology opens opportunities. Those findings do not contradict each other, and both come from vendors selling all-in-one platforms. The evaluation's point is simpler: no source on either side measures fatigue. Two vendors agreeing is convergence, not evidence. What survives is structural. FellowScript is another app, so it will face the objection at the moment of pitch and should have an answer ready. What does not survive is presenting fatigue to leaders as a research-backed pain point.

The September and January timing windows were strengthened. The brief's sources were 2011 and 2013 practitioner posts. The counter searched for opposing evidence, found none, and in the process surfaced Lifeway Research's 2025 State of Groups study (Lifeway is a Southern Baptist publishing house with an independent research arm) and a Logos launch guide, both confirming the same fall and spring rhythm. That retires the staleness concern. The remaining gap is scope: those sources describe churches recruiting members into groups, not vendors pitching leaders. The transfer is a modest inference. The critique's suggestion to pitch in August and December, ahead of launch, is a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

Untested: SmartGroups' testimonials as a format example, which the brief never claimed as proof of conversion. "Time savings for leaders" and "deeper engagement" remain unsourced as messaging angles.

### Step 7: Objections and proof points

The free-single-group tier split. The narrow claim held: a free tier with full features and no credit card removes "it costs me money" as a leader-level objection, by simple logic. The counter's central argument, that freemium products convert only 3% to 5% of users so a free tier does not "resolve" the objection, confused removing an objection with converting revenue. The evaluation rated that argument as failing outright. Where the counter landed is on the benchmark framing. SmartGroups' App Store listing shows 15 total ratings for an app dating to 2020. Ratings are a noisy signal and iOS-only, but that is strong circumstantial evidence the app has little traction. An offer structure with no demonstrated payoff is a weak thing to copy. Whether a free group tier converts to paid church plans in this market has a weak negative signal and no positive one.

Screen-free church posture held as an existence claim. Christianity Today (an editorially independent evangelical magazine) documented in 2024 that some pastors actively discourage phones and apps in church settings. The counter's Barna/Pushpay survey shows general pro-technology sentiment but measures livestreaming, giving, and management software, not whether a pastor wants screens out during a home Bible study. "Probably a minority view" is plausible but not what the sources measure. Treat it as a segment-specific objection to have an answer for, not a market-wide barrier. Note that this and the fatigue counter-claim rest on the same single vendor-sponsored survey.

Untested: data privacy (an open-access academic article on church technology, plus New Republic and Sojourners reporting on tracking in faith apps generally, which is tangential) and doctrinal safety (the same academic article plus one passing vendor mention). Tech literacy of older congregants has no source on either side. No source describes a live first-meeting demo, pilot terms, or an onboarding script as a proof-point format.

### Step 8: The ministry-coordinator role

Untested, and the best-corroborated finding in the brief. Three independent sources across two denominations and a job board agree the coordinator implements rather than decides. The United Methodist Church's Discipleship Ministries describes the role as supporting and organizing small-group ministry in partnership with the lead pastor. A Christian Reformed Church practitioner thread describes the same scope. ChurchStaffing.com lists "Small Groups Coordinator" as a paid category distinct from "Small Groups Pastor." No source offers a coordinator-specific pitch. The brief's labeled inference stands: the coordinator is an ally to equip after the SGPP or lay leader says yes, not a first contact.

## Confidence

Confidence in the overall picture is moderate to high. The brief hedged where it needed to, and none of its eight targeted claims was contradicted by evidence. The two most reliable sources on the counter side, Faith Communities Today and Lifeway Research, either cut both ways or strengthened the original. Three of the counter's arguments failed on their own terms.

Confidence is lower on anything untested. Roughly half the brief was never adversarially probed: online communities, conferences, the coordinator role, and the privacy and doctrinal objection rows. Those are sourced but not validated.

Specific findings would move if specific evidence surfaced. A survey measuring how often churches under 360 attendees employ a paid small-group role would settle whether the job-board feed reaches small churches. Referral or install data from a faith.tools listing would settle whether it is a channel or a courtesy. Any independent measurement of tool fatigue among church leaders would restore the "another app" objection to evidence status. Any faith-specific data on volunteer leader tenure would set the size of the turnover risk. Public conversion numbers from SmartGroups or a similar freemium church app would resolve whether the free tier converts. Fresh Facebook group sizes and activity would turn Step 2 from unknown into either a channel or a dead end.

## Open questions

Whether the job-board feed reaches FellowScript's segment turns on an unmade product decision: small churches as the wedge, or groups as the wedge. Research cannot settle it.

Whether RightNow Media's monthly leader email accepts third-party content was addressed by no stage.

Whether the small-group-leader Facebook groups have any real size or activity was addressed by no stage.

Whether Mark Howell or any comparable practitioner is open to partnership was addressed by no stage.

What a faith.tools listing actually produces can only be learned by listing and measuring.

Whether a free single-group tier converts to church-level paid plans in this market has a weak negative signal and nothing positive.

Whether the EdTech champion dynamic survives volunteer turnover, and how large that turnover is for small-group leaders specifically, rests on vendor-tier sourcing.

How common screen-aversion is inside small-group meetings specifically was not measured by any source.

When a vendor should pitch relative to the September and January launch windows is an untested hypothesis.

Exhibitor or sponsor pricing at Small Group Network events, tech literacy of older congregants, and any first-meeting demo or pilot-terms format have no source at all.
