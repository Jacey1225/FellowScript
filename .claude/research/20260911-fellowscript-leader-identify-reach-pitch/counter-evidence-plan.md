# Counter-Evidence Plan: Identifying, Reaching, and Pitching FellowScript to Small-Group Leaders/Pastors

Task: `20260911-fellowscript-leader-identify-reach-pitch`
Stage: critiquing (step 4, counter-evidence scope)
Inputs read: `brief.md`, `sources.json` (26 entries), `research-plan.md`

Selection logic: claims below were chosen because they are (a) load-bearing for at least one of the
brief's three success-criteria checks (identify / reach / pitch), (b) resting on `corroboration_count`
1 or on a single commercially-interested source, or (c) stated with more confidence in the brief's
prose ("the most directly actionable," "resolves the cost objection," "durable, checkable") than the
underlying source tier supports. The brief's own "Open questions" section already flagged most of
these; this plan operationalizes a prioritized subset into concrete search strategies rather than
re-asking the same questions.

## Search angles

**C1 (job-board segment match).** Genuine counter-evidence would be data on church-size distribution
in the US relative to the small-group-role-hiring population — e.g., independent survey data (Faith
Communities Today, Lifeway Research, Barna Group all publish church-size and staffing-pattern surveys)
showing what share of US churches are small/independent/non-denominational vs. large enough to post a
paid staff role, or any source estimating what fraction of *all* churches with active small-group
programs actually have a paid small-group staff position at all. Also look for practitioner sources
describing how small/independent churches identify or credential their small-group leaders *without*
a paid staff intermediary (since that's the segment the job-board tactic likely misses). Avoid sources
that just restate "there are lots of small churches" without a staffing/role angle.

**C2 (faith.tools).** Genuine counter-evidence: any independent traffic, SEO ranking, or referral data
for faith.tools (e.g., a free-tier estimate from a tool like SimilarWeb/Ahrefs, if accessible without
paid API; Wayback Machine snapshots showing site change/growth over time; the site's own analytics if
publicly claimed anywhere) that would establish real visitor volume rather than assumed relevance from
existing. Also look for user/practitioner commentary (forum posts, reviews, social mentions)
referencing faith.tools specifically as a discovery channel — evidence that pastors/leaders actually
use it, not just that it exists. Absence of any such signal after a genuine search is itself the
relevant finding — report it as an unresolved gap, not as confirmation.

**C3 (SmartGroups free tier).** Genuine counter-evidence: case studies or practitioner commentary on
freemium/free-tier conversion rates in adjacent single-purpose church-software or small-team SaaS
categories (volunteer-scheduling apps, church-check-in apps, Bible-study apps) — ideally something
that reports churn/conversion/plateau data rather than just describing the tier's existence. Also
worth checking SmartGroups' own app-store reviews and ratings trend for indirect signal of whether the
free tier is driving meaningful usage or sitting mostly unconverted. General SaaS freemium-conversion
research (industry benchmarks, e.g. 2-5% free-to-paid conversion rates in freemium models broadly) is
a legitimate cross-domain counter-data-point if no church-specific figure exists — label it as
cross-domain per the inherited constraint.

**C4 (EdTech champion analogy).** Genuine counter-evidence: research or practitioner writing on
volunteer motivation and volunteer-driven technology adoption in nonprofit/church/civic contexts
(nonprofit management literature, volunteer-engagement research from bodies like Independent Sector or
church-volunteer-retention studies) that would show whether unpaid volunteers behave like paid
institutional staff as "champions" — e.g., whether volunteers have any comparable incentive, standing,
or procurement leverage to advocate a tool upward, or whether volunteer churn/turnover undermines the
champion model in ways a salaried teacher doesn't face. Do not just re-search EdTech sales content
again — the counter-evidence must come from volunteer/nonprofit-adoption literature, not more sales
copy for a different vertical.

**C5 ("another app" fatigue).** Genuine counter-evidence: any source, ideally non-vendor, that
documents church staff/leaders *embracing* additional single-purpose tools without fatigue, or survey
data on church technology-adoption sentiment (Barna Group and Lifeway Research have both published
church-tech-attitude surveys) that either confirms or complicates the "consolidation fatigue" framing.
Also worth checking whether other vendor blogs making the identical "reduce tool sprawl" pitch
independently cite any real church survey data, or whether they all trace back to the same
unsubstantiated narrative — if every source is a vendor blog repeating the same claim, report that
convergence-without-independent-data explicitly rather than treating repetition as corroboration.

**C6 (screen-free-church posture).** Genuine counter-evidence/context: survey or practitioner data on
how widespread anti-phone/anti-app sentiment actually is among pastors broadly, versus the
Christianity Today piece being one advocacy voice in a niche movement — e.g., Barna/Lifeway surveys on
church technology adoption rates, evidence of how common in-service app use (sermon-note apps,
giving apps, check-in apps) already is in mainstream churches, which would suggest the screen-free
stance is a minority position rather than representative. Also look for counter-pieces or rebuttals to
the screen-free-church argument from other faith-media outlets.

**C7 (Sept/Jan timing).** Genuine counter-evidence: any more recent (last ~5 years, per the inherited
recency constraint) source on church engagement/marketing timing that either confirms or displaces the
Sept/Jan pattern, and — more importantly — any source specifically about *software/tool* adoption
timing in churches (vendor case studies mentioning seasonal sign-up spikes, app-store ranking
seasonality for church apps) rather than congregant-recruitment marketing. If no tool-adoption-specific
timing source exists after a genuine search, that absence should be reported, not papered over by
continuing to lean on the two recruitment-era sources.

**C8 (vendor-reported scale numbers).** Genuine counter-evidence: independent, third-party
verification or contradiction of ChurchTrac's 14,000+ churches or RightNow Media's 30,000+
churches/4M+ users claims — e.g., app-store install-count ranges, independent church-tech market-share
reports (if any exist, such as from a research firm covering the ChMS/church-software market),
review-count-to-customer-count sanity checks, or any journalism/analyst coverage that estimates these
vendors' actual scale differently. If no independent figure exists, note explicitly that these numbers
remain unverifiable rather than re-asserting them as scale evidence.

## Constraints

Inherited from `research-plan.md`:
- Prefer material from the last ~5 years for platform/channel specifics; older sourcing is acceptable
  only for durable behavioral/psychology claims — this cuts against re-using the same 2011/2013
  sources for C7 without at least attempting to find something more current.
- Every load-bearing counter-claim must be traceable to a specific, named, checkable source — no
  restating another unsourced blog claim as counter-evidence.
- Cross-domain analogies (nonprofit/volunteer literature for C4, general SaaS freemium benchmarks for
  C3) are acceptable when no faith-specific counter-evidence exists, but must be explicitly labeled
  cross-domain, matching the brief's own convention.
- Do not fabricate faith-specific counter-precedent where none exists — an honest "no counter-evidence
  found, claim stands unchallenged" is an acceptable and expected outcome for some of these targets.

Specific to counter-evidence gathering:
- Do not treat another instance of the same claim type (e.g., a second vendor blog repeating "tool
  fragmentation is bad," or a second small-group ministry blog repeating the Sept/Jan timing claim) as
  counter-evidence — that is corroboration of the original claim, not a challenge to it. Genuine
  counter-evidence must complicate, contradict, bound, or supply independent verification/refutation
  of the original claim, not restate it from a different URL.
- Prefer independent research bodies (Barna Group, Lifeway Research, Faith Communities Today, Pew
  Research on religion) and independent journalism over vendor blogs when searching for counter- or
  context-setting evidence, since the original claims already lean heavily on vendor and
  practitioner-blog sources.
- Where the honest result of a search is "no independent data exists to confirm or deny this," record
  that as the finding — this mirrors the sourcing stage's own standard of flagging absence rather than
  papering over it.

## Success criteria

A good-faith counter-evidence effort for this task looks like:
- For each of the 8 target claims, at least one genuine search attempt using the angle(s) above, with
  either (a) a named, checkable counter-source found and reported, or (b) an explicit, honest record
  that no counter-evidence exists after a real search — not a search skipped or a claim rubber-stamped
  as correct by default.
- Counter-evidence that actually bears on segment mismatch, unproven conversion/effectiveness, or
  scope mismatch (volunteer vs. paid-professional, congregant-recruitment vs. tool-pitching,
  advocacy-piece vs. representative-survey) — not superficial keyword-opposite searches (e.g. searching
  "free tiers convert well" instead of looking for actual conversion data).
- Clear labeling throughout of source tier (independent research body / independent journalism /
  competitor's own materials / vendor blog / practitioner blog) so the critique stage can weigh
  counter-evidence quality the same way the brief weighed original-evidence quality.
- At least the two highest-priority targets — C1 (job-board segment mismatch, since it undercuts the
  "Identify" success criterion for FellowScript's actual audience) and C3 (SmartGroups free-tier
  effectiveness, since it undercuts the one concrete offer-structure recommendation in "Pitch") —
  should receive the most thorough search effort, as they are the claims most directly load-bearing
  for FellowScript's next actions.
