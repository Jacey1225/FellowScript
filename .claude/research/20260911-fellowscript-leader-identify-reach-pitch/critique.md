## Per-claim verdicts

### C1 — Job-board category pages as an identification feed

**Original:** A church posting a Small Group Pastor job has decided small groups warrant a paid owner; the ChurchStaffing.com category page is therefore a durable, checkable identification feed (corroborated by ZipRecruiter).

**Counter:** 70% of US congregations have 100 or fewer weekly attendees (Faith Communities Today 2020), so the numerical majority of churches likely have no paid small-group role and are invisible to this feed. Small churches route leader identification through the pastor/deacons and an apprenticeship pipeline, not a job posting. Confidence 0.7.

**Scrutiny of the counter:** The counter's own primary source cuts both ways, and the counter did not engage with that. The same FCT report says those 70% of small congregations account for only **14% of all worshippers**. If FellowScript's unit of adoption is a *group* (up to 8 members), the population of groups is concentrated where the attendees are: the 30% of churches with more than 100 attendees, which is exactly the population the job-board feed samples from. The counter's framing ("the majority of churches") treats churches as the unit; the brief's model treats groups as the unit. On group count, the feed is far less biased than the counter implies. Second, the "small/independent church" target segment the counter is defending is an *assumption in the research plan*, not a finding from any source — the counter is measuring a mismatch against an unverified premise. Third, the counter's third source (ChurchLeaders leader-recruitment guide) attacks a claim the brief never made: the brief already said lay-leader identification does not scale via job boards; the feed is for the SGPP role.

**Scrutiny of the original:** The Lifeway 2025 figure (70-80% of ministerial staff work at congregations under 360 attendance) shows paid staff are common at mid-size churches, but a *small-group-specific* paid role is a narrower thing, and neither side measured how far down the size distribution it appears. The brief's own Risks section flagged this; the counter quantified the outer bound of the blind spot without measuring the blind spot itself. Also: ZipRecruiter "independently corroborates" the mechanic, but aggregator job boards syndicate many of the same postings — it is corroboration that the mechanic exists, not a second independent population of churches.

**Verdict:** **Original holds as a mechanic; counter holds as a scoping limit; the counter's implied conclusion (wrong feed for the target) is overstated.** The feed is a valid, checkable way to find churches with a staffed SGPP role, and those churches hold the majority of worshippers and (by inference) of groups. What the counter genuinely establishes is that this feed cannot be the *only* identification mechanic if FellowScript decides its wedge is sub-100-attendee churches — but that decision has not been made or evidenced.

### C2 — faith.tools as "the most directly actionable reach item"

**Original:** faith.tools is a free, self-serve, church-leader-facing directory of 50+ small-group apps with a paid sponsorship option; the most directly actionable reach item in the source set.

**Counter:** Similarweb estimates rank ~705k globally, ~6.9k in its category, 25% month-over-month decline, 34-second average visit; the site's Substack newsletter has "hundreds" of subscribers. Low and shrinking audience. Confidence 0.55.

**Scrutiny of the counter:** The counter contradicts a claim the brief did not make. "Most directly actionable" is a statement about cost and friction (free, self-serve, no gatekeeper), not about audience size — and the brief's own open question #2 asked precisely whether it is "real reach or just a listing." On the evidence itself: Similarweb free-tier estimates for a site ranked ~705k are noisy enough that a 25% monthly swing is within estimation error, and a 34-second average visit is normal for a directory whose job is to send visitors *out* to app listings — it is not evidence of disengagement. That said, the two counter sources (Similarweb estimate; the site's own newsletter tier) are independent of each other and agree on direction, so "small audience" is credible even if the exact numbers are not.

**Scrutiny of the original:** The brief called it "the most directly actionable" without qualifying that "actionable" and "high-yield" are different properties, which invited exactly this counter. **[critique inference]** A directory browsed by pastors specifically looking for small-group apps is high-intent traffic; at "hundreds" of newsletter subscribers and low site traffic, a listing is worth doing because it costs nothing, not because it will move adoption numbers. Neither side found any evidence of what a listing actually produces (referrals, installs), so the open question remains open.

**Verdict:** **Original holds on cost/friction; counter holds on magnitude.** Reclassify faith.tools from "most actionable reach item" to "zero-cost hygiene listing with unmeasured, probably small yield." The paid sponsorship option should not be bought on current evidence.

### C3 — SmartGroups' free-single-group tier as the offer structure to match or differentiate against

**Original:** The free single-group tier (full features, no card; church plans from $59/mo) resolves the cost-to-leader objection at exactly the leader-first entry point FellowScript targets, and is the structure to match or explicitly differentiate against.

**Counter:** SmartGroups' App Store listing shows only 15 ratings (4.6 stars), suggesting a small user base; cross-domain freemium benchmarks show 3-5% (good) to 8-12% (great) free-to-paid conversion, with a quarter of products under 2.5%. "Resolves the cost objection" overstates what has been demonstrated. Confidence 0.6.

**Scrutiny of the counter:** The counter conflates two different things. *Removing an objection* and *converting to revenue* are separate outcomes. A free tier with no card removes "it costs me money" as a leader-level objection by construction — that is a logical property of the offer, and the freemium benchmarks are irrelevant to it. The benchmarks are relevant to FellowScript's *business model* (can a free single-group tier fund the company), which is a real concern but not the claim under test. The 15-ratings figure is iOS-only, ratings are a noisy proxy (the counter concedes this), and the same URL is the brief's own source, so this is a different fact extracted from the same origin, not independent evidence.

**Scrutiny of the original:** Where the counter genuinely lands is on the word "match." The brief treats SmartGroups as "FellowScript's closest named comparable" and implicitly as a model. 15 App Store ratings for an app launched in 2020 (per its app ID era) is strong circumstantial evidence that SmartGroups has *not* achieved meaningful traction — which means its offer structure has no demonstrated payoff and is a weak thing to copy. The brief's own open question #4 anticipated this, but the summary text still reads as an endorsement.

**Verdict:** **Original's narrow claim holds (a free tier removes the leader-level cost objection). Original's "closest comparable / match" framing is weakened: SmartGroups is a small player, not a proven benchmark. Counter holds on "not proven to convert" and contributes a useful new fact (SmartGroups' low traction), but its central argument rests on a conflation.** The practical takeaway: the free-tier structure is sound as objection handling; do not treat SmartGroups' choice of it as validation, and treat free-to-paid conversion as an unmeasured risk to FellowScript's own model.

### C4 — EdTech teacher-champion mapping onto lay small-group leaders

**Original [reasoned inference, brief's own]:** teacher → lay leader, district → church/group, superintendent → SGPP/lead pastor.

**Counter:** Volunteers are intrinsically motivated (BMC Psychology systematic review) rather than institutionally incentivized like a salaried teacher; small-group and youth ministry are cited as the highest-turnover church volunteer roles (FlockConnect/Blevins), and US formal volunteering fell from 30% to 23.2% between 2019 and 2021. This bounds the transfer without invalidating it. Confidence 0.55.

**Scrutiny of the counter:** The motivation argument cuts ambiguously and the counter did not consider the other direction. The EdTech sources' point is that a champion's *peer endorsement* outweighs any vendor pitch precisely because it is not commercially motivated. A mission-driven volunteer who genuinely likes a tool is, if anything, a *more* credible peer endorser than a salaried teacher — intrinsic motivation is a feature of the champion dynamic, not a bug. The turnover argument is the stronger one, but its evidentiary tier is weak: FlockConnect appears to be a church-software vendor blog (not independently verified), and the "highest-turnover role" claim is asserted rather than measured. The 30% → 23.2% national figure is real (AmeriCorps/Census CPS) but 2021 was a pandemic year; **[critique inference, not in either source set]** the 2023 CPS supplement reported a rebound to roughly 28%, so the trend line the counter implies is not established.

**Scrutiny of the original:** The brief labeled this as its own inference and raised the exact concern in open question #5, so it was not overstated. But the brief missed a structural difference that actually favors FellowScript: in EdTech the teacher-champion *cannot buy*; procurement sits with the district. In FellowScript's model (per the brief's own hierarchy diagram), the lay leader's buy-in "unlocks a group" — the champion is much closer to the adoption decision than a teacher is. The analogy is *harder* than FellowScript's case on procurement and *easier* on champion durability. Neither side found faith-specific evidence of a champion-driven adoption pattern, which is what would settle it.

**Verdict:** **Unresolved.** The mapping survives as a labeled heuristic; the counter's turnover point is a genuine durability risk (a group's adoption may lapse when its leader rotates out), and that risk should be carried forward. The motivation point does not weaken the model. Confidence in the mapping as *guidance* is unchanged; confidence in it as *evidence* was never high and stays there.

### C5 — "Another app" / tool-fragmentation fatigue as a documented objection

**Original:** Subsplash reports staff/leader decision fatigue from managing disconnected tools — supports consolidation positioning. Brief flags it as vendor-sourced and the only source.

**Counter:** Barna-fielded, Pushpay-commissioned 2026 State of Church Technology survey (n=1,306 leaders) finds 95% say technology opens ministry opportunities and 79% say it helped congregations feel more connected; frames the challenge as "alignment," not fatigue. No independent, non-vendor survey measures app fatigue as a metric. Subsplash and Pushpay converge on the same argument without independent data. Confidence 0.45.

**Scrutiny of the counter:** The Barna figures do not contradict the fatigue claim — they measure something else. "Technology opens opportunities" and "we have too many disconnected tools" are fully compatible; a leader can hold both views. Further, the survey's "alignment" framing is Pushpay's consolidation pitch in different vocabulary (Pushpay sells an all-in-one platform), so the counter's headline source is itself a vendor-sponsored artifact of the same narrative it is being used to complicate. The counter's methodological point, however, is correct and important: there is no independent measurement of fatigue on *either* side, and Subsplash + Pushpay agreeing is convergence, not corroboration.

**Scrutiny of the original:** The brief's word "documented" is doing too much work. What is documented is that vendors *assert* fatigue; no source *measures* it. The brief's Risks section conceded the vendor bias but the summary and objection table still present it as evidence.

**Verdict:** **Original is downgraded from "documented objection" to "vendor-asserted, unmeasured objection." Counter does not falsify it; the counter's own primary source is compromised in the same way.** **[critique inference]** Independently of any survey, "another app" is an objection FellowScript will face structurally at the point of pitch simply because it *is* another app — so preparing for it is warranted regardless of population-level prevalence. What is not warranted is presenting fragmentation fatigue to leaders as a research-backed pain point.

### C6 — Screen-free-church posture as an objection

**Original:** Some pastors actively discourage phone/app use in church settings and treat technology invitations as distraction sources (Christianity Today, 2024).

**Counter:** Minority/niche advocacy position, not a mainstream trend — same Barna 2026 figures (79%/95%); Premier NexGen piece explicitly rebuts screen-free arguments on accessibility grounds. Confidence 0.6.

**Scrutiny of the counter:** The original claim is an existence claim ("some pastors"), and nothing in the counter contradicts it. The counter is answering the brief's open question #6 (prevalence), which is legitimate, but the evidence is indirect: the Barna survey measures leader sentiment toward church technology *generally* (livestreaming, giving, management software), not toward phones being out during a small-group Bible study — which is the specific context FellowScript operates in. A pastor can be enthusiastic about church tech and still not want screens in the room during a group. The Premier NexGen rebuttal is evidence the question is *contested* in faith media, which supports "exists" and "not universal" equally. Note also that the counter side reuses the same Barna/Pushpay source for both C5 and C6; two counter-claims trace to one vendor-sponsored origin.

**Scrutiny of the original:** Sound as stated. The brief did not claim prevalence and flagged that prevalence was unknown.

**Verdict:** **Original holds. Counter's "minority" characterization is plausible and probably right, but its evidence measures a different thing than the objection at issue.** Prevalence of screen-aversion *in the small-group-meeting context specifically* remains unmeasured by either side. Treat it as a segment-specific objection to have an answer for, not as a blanket market barrier.

### C7 — September and January as highest-attention windows

**Original:** Two faith-specific practitioner sources (2011, 2013) identify September and January; scope caveat that they describe congregant recruitment, not vendor pitching.

**Counter:** Honest absence. Newer sources (Lifeway Research 2025 State of Groups; Logos launch guide) confirm the same fall/spring semester cadence for congregant recruitment and still say nothing about vendor-to-leader timing. Confidence 0.2.

**Scrutiny:** The counter search *strengthened* the original. The brief's open question #7 was whether the claim is durable given 11-15-year-old sources; Lifeway Research 2025 is an independent research body confirming the same cadence, which retires the staleness risk. The scope gap (congregant recruitment vs. vendor pitch) is real but neither side found anything against the transfer, and the transfer is a modest inference. **[critique inference]** If groups launch in September and January, leaders are recruited, trained, and setting up logistics in the weeks *before* those months — so the vendor pitch window is plausibly August and December, not the launch months themselves. This is unsourced and should be carried forward as a hypothesis.

**Verdict:** **Original holds and is upgraded on recency. Counter correctly reports no contradiction.** Scope transfer remains inference.

### C8 — Vendor-reported scale figures (ChurchTrac 14,000+; RightNow Media 30,000+ churches / 4M+ users)

**Original:** Reports the figures and explicitly labels them self-reported, unaudited, falsifiable claims rather than verified facts.

**Counter:** No independent verification exists; third-party listings reference ChurchTrac at "33,000+" and "60,000+ church leaders" (URLs unconfirmed); independent ChMS market-size estimates range from ~$232M to over $1B, so no external benchmark exists. Confidence 0.35.

**Scrutiny of the counter:** This counter fails on its own terms. (1) "14,000+ churches" and "33,000-60,000 church leaders" are different units — multiple leaders per church — and the counter itself acknowledges this; a unit mismatch is not a discrepancy. The URLs for those figures were not confirmed. (2) Analyst disagreement on the dollar size of the ChMS *market* says nothing about the accuracy of one vendor's *customer count*; it is a non-sequitur presented as evidence. (3) "No independent verification" restates the brief's own Risks entry verbatim in substance. Nothing here moves the claim.

**Scrutiny of the original:** The brief's hedge is appropriate. The more damaging critique is one the counter did not make: **the figures are operationally hollow as an identification tactic.** Even if both numbers are exact, neither vendor publishes a customer list, so "customer base as proxy" identifies no specific church. The brief noted this in passing but still lists it under Identification mechanics as if it were one.

**Verdict:** **Original holds as stated (it was already hedged). Counter does not hold beyond restating the brief's own caveat; its discrepancy and market-size arguments do not survive.** The claim should be relocated from "identification mechanic" to "market-size context."

## Claims that hold

- **C6** Screen-aversion exists among some pastors and is a real objection to prepare for.

- **C8** Vendor scale figures as hedged, self-reported claims.
- The Step 8 ministry-coordinator finding (implements, doesn't decide) was not targeted and stands on three independent sources (UMC, CRC, ChurchStaffing).

**Counter side (2 hold cleanly, 4 hold partially):**

- **C1 counter** (holds) The job-board feed structurally misses the numerical majority of US churches; FCT and Lifeway are independent, high-reliability sources.
- **C2 counter** (holds) faith.tools' audience is small — two independent indicators agree on direction.
- **C3 counter** (partial) SmartGroups shows no evidence of traction (15 iOS ratings), so its offer structure is not a validated benchmark.
- **C4 counter** (partial) Lay-leader turnover is a durability risk for any champion-based adoption model.
- **C5 counter** (partial) No independent measurement of tool fatigue exists; Subsplash + Pushpay is convergence, not corroboration.
- **C6 counter** (partial) Screen-free advocacy is contested within faith media and probably a minority view.

## Claims that don't hold

- **C5 original, as worded.** "Tool-fragmentation decision fatigue *reported* among church staff/leaders" presented in the objection table as documented evidence. What exists is a vendor assertion with no measurement. Falsified as "documented"; survives only as "plausible, unmeasured."
- **C8 counter.** The ChurchTrac "discrepancy" is a units mismatch (churches vs. leaders) the counter itself concedes; the ChMS market-size spread is irrelevant to a customer count. Neither argument undermines the brief's (already hedged) claim.
- **C3 counter's central argument.** "Free tier doesn't resolve the cost objection because freemium converts at 3-5%" conflates removing a leader's objection with converting that leader to paid revenue. The conversion benchmarks bear on FellowScript's business model, not on the objection.
- **C1 counter's implied conclusion** ("wrong feed for the target segment"). Overstated: the counter's own FCT source places 86% of worshippers in the churches the feed does sample, and the "small-church target" is an unverified plan assumption.
- **C2 original's superlative** ("the most directly actionable reach item"). Not false, but it implies yield that no source supports; the counter's traffic data, noisy as it is, points the other way.
- **C4 counter's motivation argument.** Intrinsic motivation does not weaken a peer-champion model; if anything it strengthens the credibility of the endorsement. Only the turnover half of the counter survives.
- **Original's Step 1 placement of vendor customer bases as an "identification mechanic."** Accurate numbers, but with no customer list they identify nobody. Should be reclassified as market-size context.

## Unresolved

1. **Does the job-board feed reach FellowScript's segment?** Turns on a product decision (church-count wedge vs. group-count wedge) that no source can make. Both sides agree the small-group-specific paid role's prevalence below ~360 attendance is unmeasured.
2. **What does a faith.tools listing actually produce?** No referral, install, or conversion data on either side. Only resolvable by listing and measuring.
3. **Does the champion dynamic survive volunteer turnover?** No faith-specific champion-adoption evidence exists. The turnover risk is real but its magnitude for small-group leaders specifically rests on a vendor-tier blog.
4. **Is tool-fragmentation fatigue real at the population level?** Neither side has an independent measurement; both sides' sources are vendor-sponsored.
5. **How common is screen-aversion inside small-group meetings specifically?** The Barna survey measures general church-tech sentiment, not in-room phone posture during groups.
6. **When should a vendor pitch a leader relative to the Sept/Jan launch windows?** Untested; the pre-launch (August/December) hypothesis is critique inference only.
7. **Does a free single-group tier convert to church-level paid plans in this market?** SmartGroups' low traction is a weak negative signal; no positive signal exists.

- Original side's Small Group Network claims (not targeted) carry corroboration_count 2 from two pages of the same site — not independent.
- Original and counter both draw on the same SmartGroups App Store listing for C3; different facts, same origin.
