# Diff Evaluation: Identifying, Reaching, and Pitching FellowScript to Small-Group Leaders/Pastors

Task: `20260911-fellowscript-leader-identify-reach-pitch`
Stage: evaluating diffs (step 7, diff-evaluation gate)
Inputs: `critique.md` (8 per-claim verdicts), `brief.md` + `sources.json` (original side, 26 entries), `counter-claims.json` (opposing side, 8 target claims)

Method: the brief's structure (Steps 1-8, then Risks/Open questions) is walked in order. For each claim or cluster, the verdict states which side's evidence is stronger and why, then lists claims that are disproven, false, or unresolved. Verdicts are drawn only from the four input files; no new evidence is introduced. Claims the counter-evidence stage did not target are recorded as *uncontested* rather than "stronger-original" — they were never adversarially tested and should not be read as having survived one.

Category definitions used below:
- **Stronger-original / stronger-counter** — on the contested point, that side's evidence is more reliable, more independent, or more direct.
- **Disproven** — the other side's evidence directly and credibly contradicts the claim.
- **False** — contradicted by strong, reliable evidence (or by the claimant's own concession) with no credible support remaining. Stricter than "outweighed."
- **Unresolved** — genuinely inconclusive after critique; carried forward as an open question.

## Step 1 — Identification mechanics

### C1: Job-board category page as a durable identification feed

**Stronger side: original, on the mechanic. Counter, on scoping — but its implied conclusion is overstated.**

Why: The original's claim is a mechanic ("a church posting this job has decided small groups warrant a paid owner; the category page is a checkable feed"). Nothing in the counter contradicts that. The counter's two headline sources (Faith Communities Today 2020; Lifeway Research 2025) are the highest-reliability, most independent sources in either set, and they do establish that 70% of US congregations have ≤100 attendees and are structurally invisible to this feed. But the same FCT figure shows those churches hold only 14% of worshippers — so on a group-count basis (FellowScript's unit of adoption per the brief's own hierarchy), the feed samples where most groups actually are. The counter's "wrong feed for the target segment" conclusion rests on a research-plan *assumption* (small/independent churches as the wedge) that no source on either side verifies. The counter's third source (ChurchLeaders recruitment guide; CSBC guidance) attacks lay-leader identification, which the brief already conceded does not scale via job boards.

The original's ZipRecruiter "corroboration" is weaker than the brief presents: aggregator boards syndicate the same postings, so it corroborates that the mechanic exists, not a second church population. That does not change the verdict.

- **Disproven:** none on the original side. Counter's implied conclusion ("wrong feed for the target") does not hold — its own FCT source cuts against it.
- **False:** none.
- **Unresolved:** whether the feed reaches FellowScript's segment. This turns on an unmade product decision (church-count wedge vs. group-count wedge). Both sides agree the prevalence of a *small-group-specific* paid role below ~360 attendance is unmeasured.

## Step 3 — Curriculum and small-group-network ecosystems

### C8: Vendor-reported scale figures (ChurchTrac 14,000+; RightNow Media 30,000+ / 4M+)

**Stronger side: original.**

Why: The brief already presents both figures as self-reported, unaudited, falsifiable claims. The counter's three arguments fail on inspection: (1) the "discrepancy" between "14,000+ churches" and "33,000-60,000 church leaders" is a units mismatch — multiple leaders per church — which the counter's own reliability notes concede, and the URLs behind those figures were never confirmed; (2) analyst disagreement on total ChMS *market* dollar size (~$232M-$1B+) is a non-sequitur with respect to one vendor's *customer count*; (3) "no independent verification exists" restates the brief's Risks entry. Counter confidence was self-rated 0.35 and the critique reduced it to 0.15; that reduction is warranted.

- **Disproven:** none on the original side.
- **False:** the counter's "discrepancy" claim as stated — contradicted by its own concession on units and unconfirmed sourcing, with no credible support remaining. The market-size argument is not false so much as irrelevant.
- **Unresolved:** the true accuracy of either vendor figure remains unverified on both sides — but that was the brief's position from the start, so nothing moves.

### C2: faith.tools as "the most directly actionable reach item"

**Stronger side: split. Original on cost/friction; counter on magnitude. On the superlative as worded, counter.**

Why: The original's factual content (free, self-serve submission; paid sponsorship option; church-leader-facing; 50+ small-group apps) is uncontested and checkable. The superlative "most directly actionable" was never a claim about audience size, but it invited a yield reading the brief did not support. The counter's two indicators — a Similarweb free-tier estimate (global rank ~705k, 25% MoM decline, 34-second visits) and the site's own Substack tier ("hundreds" of subscribers) — are independent of each other and agree on direction. The exact Similarweb magnitudes are unreliable at that rank (a 25% monthly swing is within estimation noise; 34-second visits are normal for an outbound directory), so the counter is credible on "small," not on the specific numbers.

Net: the listing is worth doing because it costs nothing and reaches a high-intent persona, not because it will move adoption. The paid sponsorship option should not be bought on current evidence. Reclassify from "most directly actionable reach item" to "zero-cost hygiene listing with unmeasured, probably small yield."

- **Disproven:** none. The superlative is outweighed, not contradicted.
- **False:** none.
- **Unresolved:** what a listing actually produces (referrals, installs). No data on either side; resolvable only by listing and measuring.

### C4: Teacher-champion → lay-leader mapping [brief's own reasoned inference]

**Stronger side: neither. Unresolved.**

Why: The counter's motivation argument (BMC Psychology systematic review: volunteers are intrinsically motivated) is a reliable source pointed in an ambiguous direction. The EdTech champion dynamic depends on peer endorsement being non-commercial; a mission-driven volunteer who genuinely likes a tool is at least as credible an endorser as a salaried teacher. The motivation half of the counter therefore does not weaken the mapping. The turnover half (small-group/youth ministry as highest-turnover volunteer roles; US formal volunteering 30% → 23.2% 2019-2021) is the real contribution, but its faith-specific source is a church-software vendor blog (FlockConnect) plus a practitioner blog, and the national figure is a pandemic-year reading whose trend line is not established in either side's sources. The critique's note about a 2023 rebound is labeled critique inference and is not relied on here.

The original was correctly labeled as inference and pre-flagged the exact concern (open question #5), so it was not overstated. The critique adds a structural observation neither side sourced: in EdTech the champion cannot buy (procurement sits with the district), whereas in FellowScript's model the lay leader's buy-in unlocks a group — the analogy is harder on procurement and easier on champion durability than FellowScript's actual case.

- **Disproven:** none on the original side. The counter's motivation argument does not hold as a weakening of the model.
- **False:** none.
- **Unresolved:** whether the champion dynamic survives volunteer turnover. No faith-specific champion-adoption evidence exists on either side. The turnover risk is real and should be carried forward as a durability concern (a group's adoption may lapse when its leader rotates out), but its magnitude for small-group leaders specifically rests on vendor-tier sourcing.

## Step 6 — Pitch content and messaging angles

### C5: Tool-fragmentation fatigue as a "documented" objection (Subsplash)

**Stronger side: counter, on the methodological point. Original survives in a reduced form.**

Why: The counter's headline evidence (Barna-fielded, Pushpay-commissioned 2026 survey: 95% say technology opens opportunities, 79% say it helped connection) does not contradict fatigue — those are compatible sentiments, and "alignment" is Pushpay's own consolidation vocabulary, so the counter's primary source is compromised in the same way as the original's. But the counter's core point is correct and the original has no answer to it: no source on either side *measures* fatigue. Subsplash asserts it; Pushpay asserts it; two vendors selling all-in-one platforms agreeing is convergence, not corroboration. The brief's word "documented" is not supported, and the objection table presents a vendor assertion as evidence despite the Risks section's hedge.

What survives: "another app" is an objection FellowScript will structurally face at the point of pitch simply because it *is* another app — so preparing for it is warranted regardless of population prevalence (critique inference, adopted here as reasoning rather than evidence). What does not survive: presenting fragmentation fatigue *to leaders* as a research-backed pain point.

- **Disproven:** C5 original as worded ("documented objection") — not by contradiction, but by absence of any measuring source on either side, which the counter surfaced and the original cannot rebut. Downgrade to "vendor-asserted, unmeasured."
- **False:** none.
- **Unresolved:** whether tool-fragmentation fatigue is real at the population level. Both sides' sources are vendor-sponsored.

### C7: September and January as highest-attention windows

**Stronger side: original — strengthened by the counter search.**

Why: The counter reported an honest absence (confidence 0.2) and in doing so surfaced Lifeway Research 2025 (State of Groups) and a Logos launch guide, both confirming the same fall/spring cadence for congregant recruitment. Lifeway is an independent research body, more recent than the 2011/2013 originals, and independent of every original-side source. This retires the staleness risk raised in open question #7. The scope gap (congregant recruitment vs. vendor-to-leader pitch timing) remains, but neither side found anything against the transfer and it is a modest inference.

- **Disproven:** none.
- **False:** none.
- **Unresolved:** when a vendor should pitch relative to the Sept/Jan launch windows. The critique's pre-launch (August/December) hypothesis is unsourced and should be treated as a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

### Peer-testimonial format (SmartGroups); unsourced value-proposition angles

**Uncontested.** Not targeted. The brief already scoped the testimonial finding as a format example, not evidence of conversion. "Time-savings for leaders" and "deepening engagement" remain unsourced on both sides.

## Step 7 — Objection handling and proof points

### C3: SmartGroups' free-single-group tier as the structure to match or differentiate against

**Stronger side: split. Original on the narrow claim; counter on the "benchmark" framing.**

Why: The narrow claim — a free tier with full features and no card removes "it costs me money" as a leader-level objection — is a logical property of the offer and holds regardless of evidence. The counter's central argument (freemium converts at 3-5% good / 8-12% great / a quarter under 2.5%, therefore the free tier does not "resolve" the objection) conflates removing an objection with converting a user to revenue. Those benchmarks bear on FellowScript's business model, not on the objection under test; they are also cross-domain (Userpilot SaaS aggregator), which the counter itself labels.

Where the counter lands: the brief calls SmartGroups "FellowScript's closest named comparable" and implicitly treats its offer structure as a model to "match." The App Store listing shows 15 total ratings for an app whose ID dates to 2020. That is iOS-only and ratings are a noisy proxy (the counter concedes), and it is the same URL the brief cites (a different fact from the same origin, not independent evidence) — but it is strong enough circumstantial evidence that SmartGroups has not achieved meaningful traction. An offer structure with no demonstrated payoff is a weak thing to copy. The brief's open question #4 anticipated this, but the summary text still reads as endorsement.

- **Disproven:** the counter's central argument ("free tier doesn't resolve the cost objection because freemium converts poorly") — it does not hold; the conflation leaves no credible support for the "doesn't resolve" claim as directed at the objection.
- **False:** none.
- **Unresolved:** whether a free single-group tier converts to church-level paid plans in this market. SmartGroups' low traction is a weak negative signal; no positive signal exists.

### C6: Screen-free-church posture as an objection (Christianity Today, 2024)

**Stronger side: original.**

Why: The original is an existence claim ("some pastors actively discourage phone/app use in church settings"), sourced to an editorially independent publication. The counter does not contradict existence; it answers prevalence (open question #6). Its evidence is indirect: the Barna/Pushpay survey measures general church-tech sentiment (livestreaming, giving, management software), not in-room phone posture during a small-group Bible study. A pastor can favor church tech and still not want screens out during a group. The Premier NexGen rebuttal shows the position is contested within faith media, which supports "exists" and "not universal" equally. The counter's "minority/niche" characterization is plausible and probably right, but it is not what its sources measure.

Independence note: this is the second counter-claim (with C5) resting on the same Barna/Pushpay origin.

- **Disproven:** none.
- **False:** none.
- **Unresolved:** how common screen-aversion is inside small-group meetings specifically. Treat as a segment-specific objection to have an answer for, not a blanket market barrier.

## Overall picture

The original brief held up well against the counter-evidence, largely because it was hedged where it needed to be. Of the eight targeted claims, none was disproven outright by contradicting evidence. The counter side's most reliable sources (FCT 2020, Lifeway Research 2025) either cut both ways (C1) or ended up strengthening the original (C7). Two of the counter side's arguments fail entirely on their own terms: the C8 "discrepancy" is a conceded units mismatch plus an irrelevant market-size spread, and the C3 central argument conflates removing an objection with converting revenue.

Where the counter genuinely moved the picture, it did so on framing rather than fact:

1. **C5** — "documented" was never supported; fragmentation fatigue is vendor-asserted and unmeasured on both sides. The objection is still worth preparing for structurally, but not worth pitching to leaders as research-backed.
2. **C2** — faith.tools is a zero-cost hygiene listing, not a reach channel. Do not buy sponsorship on current evidence.
3. **C3** — SmartGroups' free tier is sound objection handling, but SmartGroups itself is a small player with no demonstrated traction, so its structure is not a validated benchmark to "match."
4. **C8 (placement)** — vendor customer bases identify no specific church and belong under market-size context, not identification mechanics.

Two independence problems on the original side should be carried forward: ZipRecruiter partly syndicates ChurchStaffing postings (corroborates the mechanic, not a second population), and Small Group Network's corroboration_count of 2 is two pages of one site. On the counter side, C5 and C6 share a single vendor-sponsored origin (Barna/Pushpay), so the counter's "mainstream pro-tech sentiment" theme rests on one source.

Roughly half the brief was never adversarially tested. Step 2 (online communities), Step 4 (conferences), Step 8 (coordinator role), and the privacy/doctrinal objection rows were not targeted; three of the brief's nine open questions (#3, #8, #9) were not addressed by any stage. The conclusion stage should treat those as sourced-but-untested rather than as validated, and should be explicit that the most consequential unresolved item — whether FellowScript's wedge is small churches (where the job-board feed is blind) or groups (where it is well-aimed) — is a product decision, not a research gap.
